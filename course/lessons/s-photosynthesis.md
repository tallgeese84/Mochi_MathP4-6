# Photosynthesis and the need for food

## Food-making uses light energy
Green plants use light energy to make food from water and carbon dioxide through photosynthesis. Oxygen is released. Chlorophyll, the green pigment, helps absorb light energy. Leaves are common sites of photosynthesis, although other green parts can also carry it out.

Light is an energy input, not a material ingredient that becomes water. Water and carbon dioxide supply materials used to make food. The distinction between energy and matter helps explain why simply watering a plant in darkness cannot support food-making indefinitely.
## Where the inputs come from
Roots commonly absorb water, which is transported to leaves through water-carrying tubes. Carbon dioxide enters from the surrounding air. Light comes from a suitable source such as the Sun. The plant must have the necessary structures and suitable conditions to use these inputs.

Mineral salts absorbed by roots support healthy growth but are not the sugar food made by photosynthesis. Fertiliser does not replace the need for light and carbon dioxide. It is misleading to say that plants obtain all their food by “eating nutrients from soil”.
## Food is used and stored
Food made in green parts can be transported to other parts, used for growth and repair, or used in respiration to release energy. Some may be stored in forms such as starch. Roots and other non-green parts depend on this supply even though they may not photosynthesise themselves.

A plant is therefore both a food producer and a living organism that uses food. Producing food does not remove its need to release energy. This point connects plant systems, food chains and energy conversion.
## Photosynthesis and respiration differ
Photosynthesis makes food using light energy. Respiration releases energy from food in living cells. Plants respire during both day and night. Photosynthesis requires suitable light, so the balance of gas exchanges can change with conditions.

Do not memorise “plants take in carbon dioxide and animals take in oxygen” as a complete account. Plants also use oxygen in aerobic respiration. For primary questions, identify which process is being asked about before stating the gas inputs and outputs.
## Worked investigation: light
To investigate whether light is needed for photosynthesis, a classroom demonstration may compare leaf regions receiving light with regions kept dark, then test for starch after appropriate preparation. The setup must account for previously stored starch and other differences.

The chemical procedures in a starch test require trained adult supervision and are not a home activity in this app. Learn the reasoning from recorded observations or a safe classroom demonstration: a starch result is evidence about stored food under the test conditions, not a direct photograph of energy.
## Evaluate an illustration
The seedling illustration presents a possible setup, not measured results. Compare starting sizes, water, soil, temperature, duration and light. If one plant begins larger, final height alone is not a fair measure of growth. A shade box may also change temperature and air movement.

Propose improvements before predicting results. Measure change in height and other suitable signs, use similar starting plants and repeat observations. A long pale shoot in darkness does not establish that darkness produces a healthier plant; height and health are different claims.
## Explain and transfer
Explain the chain light energy enables food-making; food reaches plant parts; respiration releases energy from food; growth and other life processes use materials and energy. Keep each step distinct.

Then consider a food chain beginning with a green plant. Photosynthesis introduces a source of food for organisms that cannot make food this way. This prepares you to explain why reducing suitable light can affect organisms beyond the plants themselves.
