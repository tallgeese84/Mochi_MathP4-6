# Food chains, webs and communities

## Organisms and their surroundings
A habitat is the place where an organism lives. Physical conditions include light, temperature, water and air. Organisms also interact with other living things. A suitable habitat provides conditions and resources that meet the organism’s needs.

A population consists of organisms of the same kind living in a particular area. A community includes populations of different kinds living and interacting in that area. A pond illustration can show a community, but it does not establish exact population sizes or every interaction.
## Producers, consumers and decomposers
Green plants are producers because they make food through photosynthesis. Consumers obtain food by feeding on other organisms. Decomposers, including many fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms and wastes, helping return materials to the environment.

Decomposers are not simply “bad organisms”. Their role helps explain recycling of materials. Energy and materials must not be confused: materials cycle, while energy enters many ecosystems through sunlight and is transferred through feeding and other processes.
## Food-chain arrows
In a food chain, an arrow points from the food organism to the organism that feeds on it. It represents transfer of energy through feeding. Grass → grasshopper → frog means the grasshopper eats grass and the frog eats the grasshopper.

Do not reverse arrows to show where an animal moves while hunting. The arrow has a defined meaning. A food chain is a simplified model; many organisms have more than one food source and may be eaten by more than one kind of consumer.
## Food webs
A food web combines several feeding relationships. Follow individual arrows to identify food sources and consumers. Before predicting a change, check whether alternative foods or predators exist. Removing one food source may have a smaller effect when others remain available.

A web diagram records specified relationships; it does not automatically give feeding rates or population sizes. Avoid claiming exact numbers from arrows alone. Different time periods, movement into the area and other environmental changes can alter outcomes.
## Worked prediction
Suppose the stated chain is grass → grasshopper → frog, and frogs decrease while other conditions initially remain similar. Reduced feeding on grasshoppers may allow their population to increase. More grasshoppers may then consume more grass.

Use “may” and state the assumptions. Disease, migration, food shortage or other predators could change the outcome. This does not make the chain useless; it makes the prediction conditional on the model. Explain each link instead of writing an unsupported “everything will die”.
## Observe a habitat
Study a small safe area such as a garden patch. Record visible organisms and physical conditions without disturbing them. Use references to check identities and feeding relationships. Do not assume that two organisms near one another must eat each other.

Construct a food chain only from supported relationships. Mark unknown links as questions to research. The original pond illustration is a starting point for observation and inquiry, not a species-accurate record of a particular habitat or an experimentally established food web.
## Explain and transfer
For a population-change question, name the changed factor, trace the direct effect, then consider indirect effects and alternatives. State which relationships come from the question and which require further evidence.

Explain why protecting producers can affect consumers and why decomposition matters. This connects photosynthesis, feeding, energy transfer and recycling into a system account rather than a collection of disconnected facts.
