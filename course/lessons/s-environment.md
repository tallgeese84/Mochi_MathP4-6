# Adaptations, environmental change and evidence

## Adaptations connect features to conditions
An adaptation is a feature or behaviour that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment. Explain both the feature and its effect. A broad leaf may help capture light in a particular setting; a body covering may reduce water loss in another.

Avoid purpose-only stories such as “it has this because it wanted to survive”. Organisms do not choose to grow every useful inherited feature when they need it. Detailed evolutionary mechanisms belong to later study; here, focus on how an observed feature functions under stated conditions.
## Structural and behavioural examples
Structural adaptations involve body features; behavioural adaptations involve actions. A streamlined body can reduce resistance during movement through water. Seeking shade during the hottest part of a day can reduce exposure to heat.

One feature may help in one environment and have costs in another. A very insulating covering may be useful in cold conditions but create difficulties in heat. Do not classify an organism as perfectly adapted to every possible environment from a single feature.
## Competition and resources
Organisms compete when they need a limited resource such as food, water, light or space. Competition can occur among the same kind of organism and among different kinds. A resource being present does not mean there is enough for everyone.

For plants close together, shading can reduce light available to shorter plants, while roots may compete for water and mineral salts. An investigation must distinguish these possible causes. Spacing, water and light can change together, so a simple growth difference may not isolate one factor.
## Human impacts
Human activities can change habitats through pollution, land clearing, resource use and introduction of organisms. Effects can spread through food webs and physical conditions. A polluted water body may affect producers, consumers and people who depend on it.

Conservation aims to protect living things and the resources and conditions they need. Proposals should connect action to mechanism: protecting vegetation can preserve habitat; reducing pollutants can improve water conditions. “Save nature” expresses a goal but does not explain how a particular action helps.
## Worked decision: a school pond
Imagine a school pond with fewer visible insects after a nearby area is cleared. One explanation is habitat loss; another is seasonal change or altered observation conditions. First check how counts were made, over what duration, and whether other conditions changed.

A useful response might combine repeated standardised observations with restoring suitable vegetation. Record both the action and the predicted effect. If insect counts rise later, consider other changes before claiming the intervention alone caused the increase. Good intentions do not replace careful evidence.
## Read beyond the lesson
Choose a suitable science report from a museum, university or recognised science organisation. Identify its main claim, the evidence offered, the date and who produced it. Separate a directly measured result from an expert prediction or an illustration.

Write one paragraph explaining the idea in your own words, then list a question the report does not answer. Check that a second source actually addresses the same claim. Wider reading supports curiosity and application; it is not a requirement to memorise every current science headline for an unpublished examination blueprint.
## Explain, discuss and revise
Give a two-minute explanation of an environmental problem: the organisms affected, the changed condition, the causal links, possible actions and the evidence needed. Ask another person to challenge one assumption. Revise your explanation if the challenge reveals a gap.
