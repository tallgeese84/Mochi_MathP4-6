# Think and work like a scientist

## Start with a question
Science is a way of building and checking explanations about the natural world. A useful investigation question says what will change and what will be observed. “Do plants like music?” is unclear. “Do seedlings exposed to a particular sound grow taller than similar seedlings kept without that sound?” is more testable, although a fair investigation would still need careful planning.

An observation describes what you noticed or measured. An inference is an interpretation of those observations. “The leaf has brown edges” is an observation. “The plant lacked water” is one possible inference. Heat, disease or damaged roots might also explain the observation. Keep possible explanations open until evidence distinguishes them.
## Change, measure, keep the same
For a fair test, change the factor you are investigating, measure an outcome, and keep other factors that could affect that outcome the same. To investigate how exposed water surface area affects evaporation, use the same starting volume, water temperature, location and duration. Change the exposed surface area and measure the water remaining after a fixed time.

The changed factor is not always what you measure. If you change the number of batteries, bulb brightness is an outcome. If two important conditions change together, an observed difference cannot be confidently attributed to either one alone. State specific controls instead of writing only “make it a fair test”.
## Measure meaningfully
Choose a quantity that matches your question. Plant height, leaf number and dry mass describe different features of growth. “Healthier” is too vague unless you define what will count as evidence. Record units, starting values and the time of each observation.

Repeat measurements or use several similar specimens to see whether a pattern is consistent. Repetition helps reveal variation; it does not repair an unfair design. Three repetitions of a test that changes both light and water still cannot isolate the effect of light. Keep unusual results visible and investigate them instead of deleting them merely because they do not fit your prediction.
## Worked example: seedlings
Imagine three seedlings in each of two groups. Both groups have the same type of plant, similar starting sizes, equal soil and water, and the same duration. One receives more light. Record each plant’s starting and final height, then compare changes rather than just final heights.

Even this design has limits: a lamp may also warm one group. Check temperature or choose a setup that reduces that difference. A taller seedling is not automatically healthier; a seedling in dim light can become long and pale. A careful conclusion identifies what was measured and avoids claiming more than the evidence shows.
## Turn results into an explanation
Suppose equal volumes of water in two containers fall by 8 ml and 3 ml. Describe the pattern, identify which condition belongs to each result, then connect the pattern to a relevant scientific idea. “Under these conditions, more water evaporated from the container with the larger exposed surface area” is stronger than “my prediction was right”.

Tables preserve exact values. Graphs help reveal relationships. Put the changed numerical factor on the horizontal axis when appropriate, label quantities and units, and select a sensible scale. Do not invent values between categories such as wood and metal as though they formed a numerical sequence.
## Try a safe investigation
Fold two equal sheets of paper into different bridge shapes. Keep the distance between supports the same. Add identical light counters one at a time and record the number supported before the bridge bends to a chosen limit. Keep fingers clear of falling objects and use only a low setup.

Before testing, write your prediction and a reason. Afterward, record whether the result supports it, what could have affected the comparison, and one improvement. A prediction that turns out to be wrong can still lead to excellent science if the evidence changes your explanation.
## Explain and transfer
Use the sequence question, prediction, method, observations, explanation, limitation and next test. It is a thinking routine, not a sentence template that replaces understanding. Your explanation must match the actual investigation.

For unfamiliar problems, underline what the evidence establishes and circle what it leaves uncertain. Ask what observation would distinguish two competing explanations. This habit is useful in school questions, science reading and everyday claims about products or health.
