# Factors, multiples and systematic search

## Start with arrangements
Arrange 24 counters in equal rows. One row of 24, two rows of 12, three rows of 8 and four rows of 6 all work. Five rows do not work without a remainder. A factor divides a number exactly: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24 are factors of 24.

A multiple is a number obtained by multiplying by a whole number. The positive multiples of 6 begin 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30. Six is a factor of 24, and 24 is a multiple of six. Say the entire relationship; saying only that two numbers 'go together' hides the direction.
## A complete search
Look for factor pairs in increasing order of the smaller number. For 36, the pairs are 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9 and 6 × 6. Once the smaller and larger factors meet, later pairs would repeat the same products in reverse order. This gives a reason for stopping.

List the factors in order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Notice that 6 appears only once, even though both members of its pair are equal. A square number has a middle factor pair of this kind. You can explain that observation with a square arrangement of counters.
## Worked example: equal packs
You have 18 purple beads and 24 white beads. You want identical packs, using every bead, with the greatest possible number of packs. The number of packs must divide both 18 and 24. Their common factors are 1, 2, 3 and 6, so six packs are possible at most.

Each pack contains 18 ÷ 6 = 3 purple beads and 24 ÷ 6 = 4 white beads. Check the result by rebuilding the totals. The choice of common factors follows from sharing two whole-number quantities without leftovers; it is not just a keyword rule about 'greatest'.
## Worked example: meeting again
One light flashes every four seconds and another every six seconds. They flash together now. List future flash times: 4, 8, 12, 16… and 6, 12, 18… . Their next shared time is 12 seconds. We seek the smallest positive common multiple because we want the first meeting after now.

Zero is a common multiple too, but it describes the starting moment and cannot answer 'next'. Be explicit about the positive condition. The same reasoning applies to repeating patterns, tiles fitting a length exactly and regular schedules, as long as the repeating intervals remain constant.
## Diagnose the reasoning
A pupil chooses 18 as the greatest common factor of 18 and 24 because it is the smaller number. Test the claim: 24 ÷ 18 is not a whole number. A common factor must pass both divisibility tests. A plausible-looking answer is not enough.

Another pupil multiplies every pair of intervals to find when they meet again. The product is a common multiple, but may not be the smallest. For intervals four and six, 24 works but 12 comes earlier. Explain why shared factors can allow an earlier meeting.
## Explore with objects
Use twelve counters to make every different rectangular arrangement, counting rotated copies only once. Then try thirteen counters. Thirteen makes only a one-by-thirteen arrangement. A whole number greater than one with exactly two positive factors is called a prime number; this vocabulary is enrichment here.

Do not say one is prime: it has only one positive factor. Try numbers from 2 to 20 and record your factor pairs. The useful habit is systematic testing, not memorising a list. If someone disagrees with your classification, a factor pair can settle the disagreement.
## Explain and transfer
Suppose two repeating bells ring together every time both cycles finish. Explain how a multiples list answers that question, then explain how a factors list solves identical-pack questions. The operations may look related, but the unknown plays a different role.

For independent work, show enough of your list or factor pairs for another person to see that your search is complete. If you use a quicker method, explain why it cannot miss a better answer. That is the beginning of mathematical proof through an organised search.
