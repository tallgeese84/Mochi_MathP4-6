# Transport and breathing: connected systems

## A system serves other systems
Digested food must reach body parts, and oxygen must reach cells. Carbon dioxide produced in the body must also be transported away. The digestive, respiratory and circulatory systems work together to make these exchanges possible.

No one organ completes all these tasks. The small intestine absorbs digested food, lungs exchange gases, and the heart pumps blood through blood vessels. Linking functions is more useful than memorising organ names as an isolated list.
## Air into the lungs
Air enters through the nose or mouth, passes along the windpipe and reaches the lungs. In the lungs, oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide moves from blood into the air that is breathed out. The primary model does not require naming tiny air sacs or detailed branching structures.

Air is a mixture containing mainly nitrogen and oxygen, with smaller amounts of gases including carbon dioxide and water vapour. Breathed-out air still contains oxygen; it is not pure carbon dioxide. Relative amounts change because of gas exchange.
## Blood transports materials
The heart pumps blood through blood vessels. Blood carries oxygen from the lungs and digested food from the digestive system to body parts. It also carries carbon dioxide back toward the lungs for removal during breathing out.

Do not say that the heart makes oxygen or digests food. It enables transport by pumping. Detailed heart chambers, valves and classifications of vessels are beyond the required primary scope here, so focus on the connected routes and functions.
## Breathing and energy release
Breathing moves air into and out of the lungs. Respiration releases energy from food in living cells; it is not simply another word for breathing. Oxygen is used in the usual aerobic process, and carbon dioxide is produced. The detailed chemical equation is extension work.

During exercise, the body’s demand for energy increases. Breathing and heartbeat commonly increase to support faster supply and removal of materials. Measurements vary with effort, health and fitness, so avoid diagnosing a person from a single reading in a school activity.
## Plant transport
Roots absorb water and mineral salts. Water-carrying tubes transport these to the stem and leaves. Food made in green parts is transported through food-carrying tubes to other parts that need it, including roots and growing structures.

Water and food pathways have different roles. Food is not simply carried only upward from the soil. The specialist terms xylem and phloem can be learned later; understanding the two functions is the primary goal. Plants also release energy from food through respiration in living cells.
## Worked comparison
Consider a leafy cutting placed in coloured water in an adult-led demonstration. Colour appearing along particular paths supports a conclusion about water transport. It does not by itself show the route of all transported food, because the colour entered with water.

Compare this with a human system diagram. Arrows must be labelled with what is moving: air, blood, oxygen or digested food. Without labels, an attractive arrow diagram can wrongly imply that whole food pieces travel from the stomach into the heart.
## Explain and transfer
Explain how oxygen reaches a working leg muscle and how digested food reaches the same area. Both involve blood transport, but their starting routes differ. Then explain how a root receives food from leaves while supplying water used by them.

Use the pattern source, pathway, destination and purpose. If an organ or transport pathway is damaged, predict the consequence by following what it normally supplies. Avoid jumping directly to a disease name that the evidence does not establish.
