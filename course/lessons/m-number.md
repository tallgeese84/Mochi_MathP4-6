# Numbers have structure

## Start with a question
Imagine that a library has 406,080 books. What does each digit contribute? Reading a large number is easier when you describe its structure: four hundred thousands, no ten thousands, six thousands, no hundreds, eight tens and no ones. The number is 400,000 + 6,000 + 80. The zeros keep the other digits in their proper places; they are not invitations to close the gaps.

In this class, use a place-value chart before calculating. Draw columns for millions, hundred thousands, ten thousands, thousands, hundreds, tens and ones. Put one digit in each column. Say both the digit and its value: the digit 6 here has value 6,000. This distinction prevents many errors later with decimals and measurement.
## Build the idea
Our base-ten system exchanges ten units of one place for one unit of the next place. Ten hundreds equal one thousand. Therefore 3,000 can also be described as 30 hundreds or 300 tens. The quantity stays the same while its grouping changes. Written addition, subtraction, multiplication and division all depend on this exchange.

To compare numbers, first compare the number of digits when there are no leading zeros. For equal-length whole numbers, work from the greatest place downwards. Compare 406,080 and 460,008: the hundred-thousands digits agree, but 0 ten thousands is less than 6 ten thousands. Later digits cannot reverse that decision because their combined value is smaller than one ten thousand.
## Work through an example
Round 46,780 to the nearest thousand. It lies between 46,000 and 47,000. The halfway point is 46,500. Since 46,780 is above halfway, the nearest thousand is 47,000. Its distances are 780 and 220 respectively. This distance explanation is the reason behind the familiar rounding rule.

Now round the same number to the nearest hundred: the neighbours are 46,700 and 46,800, so choose 46,800. The requested place matters. Write 46,780 ≈ 47,000 when rounding to a thousand. The approximately-equal sign reminds us that rounding changes the number, whereas 46,780 = 46,000 + 780 is an exact statement.
## Investigate a common mistake
A pupil says that multiplying 408 by 10 means putting a zero anywhere. Explain why that cannot be right. Every quantity becomes ten times as large: 4 hundreds become 4 thousands, and 8 ones become 8 tens. Thus 408 × 10 = 4,080. The place-value change is the explanation, and the written zero is a consequence.

Rounding also does not always make a number larger. Rounding 46,210 to the nearest thousand produces 46,000. An estimate is useful for judging size, but it is not automatically an acceptable final answer. Read whether the problem asks for an exact quantity or a specified approximation.
## Try it together
A school buys 198 packs containing 21 sheets each. Before doing the exact multiplication, estimate 200 × 20 = 4,000 sheets. The exact answer should be close to that scale. An answer of 416 or 41,580 would need checking. Work out the exact answer by 198 × (20 + 1) = 3,960 + 198 = 4,158.

Explain why this estimate does not prove the exact answer. Many nearby numbers would pass the same size check. Use a second method, such as 200 × 21 − 2 × 21, to confirm 4,158. Estimation and exact verification play different roles.
## Use your notebook
Make cards for 0, 2, 5 and 8. Form the greatest and smallest four-digit numbers using each card once; do not begin with zero. Explain the first position you chose and why it controls the result. Then round both numbers to the nearest hundred and compare the rounding errors.

Pause before checking: the greatest number is 8,520; the smallest is 2,058. Their nearest hundreds are 8,500 and 2,100. Explain why simply sorting all digits upwards would fail for a four-digit number. This is a small example of solving a problem while respecting constraints.
## Explain and transfer
Teach someone the difference between a number, a digit and a place value. Give an example where the same digit has two different values. Then read a large number from a newspaper or a school report and identify whether it is an exact count or an estimate.

Before independent practice, check that you can explain the halfway point used in rounding, regroup a thousand as hundreds, and reject an answer with an implausible size. If one idea remains uncertain, revisit the place-value chart. Being able to recite a rule is less useful than being able to reconstruct it.
