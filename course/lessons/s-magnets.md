# Magnets and magnetic interactions

## Attraction without touching
A magnet can exert a force on certain materials without touching them. Iron and many kinds of steel are familiar magnetic materials. Not every metal is attracted strongly to a classroom magnet: aluminium and copper, for example, do not behave like iron in this test.

Magnetic force is an interaction. A nearby magnet may pull an iron object toward it, even across a small gap. Whether the object actually moves also depends on other forces and on how it is supported. Observe the effect rather than assuming that no movement means no force.
## Two poles
A bar magnet has a north pole and a south pole. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Repulsion pushes the magnets apart, while attraction pulls them toward each other. The effects are usually strongest near a bar magnet’s poles.

The labels north and south do not mean positive and negative electrical charges. Keep magnetic poles and battery terminals as separate ideas. A magnet freely suspended so that it can turn tends to align roughly north–south; a compass uses this behaviour for finding direction.
## Magnet or magnetic material?
An iron object may be attracted by either pole of a magnet. Therefore attraction alone does not prove that the object is itself a permanent magnet. Repulsion between an unknown object and a known magnet is stronger evidence of magnetic poles in the unknown object.

For example, if one end of an unknown bar repels a known north pole, that end behaves as a north pole. The other end of a simple bar magnet is south. Explain the observation using like-pole repulsion, not a claim that all metal has north and south ends.
## Making a magnet
A suitable iron or steel object can be magnetised by a stroking method using a magnet. In a classroom demonstration, strokes follow a consistent direction and procedure rather than random back-and-forth rubbing. Magnetic effects can also be produced using an electric current in a coil around a suitable core.

An electromagnet can be controlled by switching its current on and off. Any practical coil activity should use an adult-approved low-voltage kit and short supervised trials: wires and batteries can heat. Never connect wires directly across a battery or use mains electricity.
## Worked investigation
To compare two bar magnets by how many identical paper clips they support, keep the clip type, contact location and method the same. Repeat the test. The result compares performance in that test; it is not a complete measurement of every possible magnetic effect.

To investigate distance, use the same magnet, object, surface and orientation while changing the gap. Do not change both magnet and gap and then attribute every difference to distance. A force can act through some non-magnetic materials, so a thin sheet between objects does not necessarily stop the effect.
## Explore safely
Use a large classroom magnet with adult supervision and a tray of suitable objects. Predict and test which are attracted, recording the material where known. Keep magnets away from electronics, medical devices and loose small objects that could be swallowed. Do not use small powerful magnets.

Separate observations from assumptions. “This object is attracted” is an observation. “It contains iron” is a possible explanation that may require product information. Colour or shininess alone cannot tell you whether a material is magnetic.
## Explain and transfer
For every pole problem, label the known pole, state attraction or repulsion, and apply the correct rule. For an unknown object, ask whether the evidence distinguishes a magnet from a magnetic material.

Useful applications connect a property to a purpose: a compass can turn to indicate direction; an electromagnet can release an attracted object when switched off. Learning the causal connection helps you reason about unfamiliar devices without memorising a list of uses.
