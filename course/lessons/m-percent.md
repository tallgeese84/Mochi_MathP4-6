# Percentages describe a part per hundred

## Name the reference quantity
Percent means per hundred. Twenty-five percent describes 25 out of every 100 equal parts of a reference quantity. It equals 25/100 = 1/4 = 0.25. A percentage is not a dollar amount until you identify the quantity that counts as 100%.

If 25% of one class is 8 pupils and 25% of another class is 10 pupils, the classes have different totals. Equal percentages do not guarantee equal counts. Always write '100% = …' beside your first model. This habit also prepares you for reverse percentages and changing quantities.
## Find a percentage part
To find 15% of $80, first find 10% = $8 and 5% = $4. Their sum is $12. Alternatively calculate 15/100 × 80 = 12. These are different descriptions of the same proportional relationship, not competing rules.

Some percentages have useful fraction forms: 50% is one half, 25% is one quarter, 20% is one fifth and 10% is one tenth. Derive each by simplifying the fraction over 100. When a percentage is unfamiliar, the unitary method of finding 1% still works.
## Worked example: a discount
A bag is priced at $60 and has a 15% discount. The discount is 15% of the original $60, or $9. The sale price is $60 − $9 = $51. Equivalently, the customer pays 85% of $60. Distinguish the amount taken off from the amount paid.

Check that the sale price is less than the original and that their difference is $9. A common error reports $9 as the price because the percentage calculation itself was correct. Solving the requested question requires one further interpretation.
## A stated tax or fee
For a practice problem, suppose a service costs $50 before a stated 8% tax. The tax is $4 and the total is $54. This is a hypothetical rate supplied by the problem, not a claim about the current Singapore GST rate. Use the rate given unless a real-world task explicitly asks you to look it up.

If a discount applies before the tax, the tax reference may be the discounted price. Write each stage in order rather than adding or subtracting percentages blindly. For example, a $100 item with 20% off becomes $80; a stated 10% tax on $80 adds $8, giving $88.
## Annual interest as an application
Simple annual interest at a stated 3% on $200 is 3/100 × 200 = $6 for one year. The interest is the extra amount, while $206 is principal plus that year's interest. Name which of these the question requests.

Do not assume every savings product works by this simplified model. A question may specify changing rates, different time periods or interest added to the balance. For core practice, use the stated annual relationship. General financial claims require the actual product's terms, beyond this mathematical example.
## Guided task and error analysis
A pupil scores 18 out of 24 on a quiz. The fraction is 18/24 = 3/4, so the percentage is 75%. Dividing 18 by 100 would answer a different question because the actual total is 24. To express a part as a percentage, divide the part by its whole, then multiply by 100%.

Compare 18/24 with 20/25. The second is 80%, so it represents a larger fraction correct even though the totals differ. Percentages provide a common scale, but they do not prove the quizzes had the same difficulty or measured the same skills.
## Explain and transfer
Choose an everyday percentage claim and identify its numerator, denominator and reference group. A percentage without a clearly defined whole can mislead. Ask whether the claim concerns the original amount, a current amount, a total population or only a selected sample.

Before practice, explain both 30% of 50 and 30 as a percentage of 50. The first asks for a part and gives 15. The second asks for a rate and gives 60%. Similar numbers can belong to different questions.
