# Why fraction division works

## Begin with sharing
A jug holds 3/4 litre of juice. Three people share it equally. Draw three quarter-litre portions and give one to each person. Each receives 1/4 litre, so 3/4 ÷ 3 = 1/4. The original quantity has been divided into three equal shares.

Now imagine only 1/4 litre shared among three people. Each quarter must be split into three equal parts. A full litre would contain twelve such parts, so each person receives 1/12 litre. This gives a reason for 1/4 ÷ 3 = 1/12. Avoid memorising a rule without knowing what the smaller pieces represent.
## Division can count groups
How many half-litre cups can be filled from three litres? There are two halves in every litre, so six half-litres fit. Thus 3 ÷ 1/2 = 6. Dividing by a proper fraction can produce a quotient larger than the starting number because the groups being counted are smaller than one unit.

Compare the two questions 'How much does each of two people receive?' and 'How many groups of size one half fit?' The first is 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 litres per person. The second is 3 ÷ 1/2 = 6 groups. The divisor has a different meaning; draw it before choosing an operation.
## Derive the method
Find 3/4 ÷ 2/3 by measuring both quantities in twelfths. Three quarters are nine twelfths; two thirds are eight twelfths. Nine of these equal units contain 9/8 groups of eight. Therefore 3/4 ÷ 2/3 = 9/8 = 1 1/8.

Algebraically, renaming both fractions in common units gives (3 × 3)/(4 × 2), the same as 3/4 × 3/2. Multiplying by the reciprocal is a compact consequence of counting equal units. Only the divisor is inverted. The dividend remains the quantity being measured.
## Work through a ribbon problem
A ribbon is 5/6 metre long. Each decoration needs 1/4 metre. The quotient 5/6 ÷ 1/4 = 20/6 = 3 1/3 tells us how many quarter-metre lengths the ribbon contains. Only three complete decorations can be made. They use 3/4 metre.

The leftover length is 5/6 − 3/4 = 1/12 metre. Notice that the fractional part of the quotient, one third, is one third of a decoration-length, not one third of a metre. Keeping units attached to the steps prevents that subtle misinterpretation.
## Repair a mistaken explanation
A pupil says division always makes a number smaller. Test 2 ÷ 1/4 with a drawing of two wholes, each split into quarters. There are eight groups. The division is correct because it asks how many small groups fit, not how to shrink a quantity.

Another pupil divides 2/5 by 3 and gets 6/5. Check by multiplying the proposed share by three: 18/5 is not 2/5. The correct share, 2/15, satisfies 3 × 2/15 = 2/5. The inverse operation is a general check that works beyond this example.
## A small investigation
Take a paper strip representing one metre. Mark halves, then quarters, then eighths. Ask how the number of groups fitting in one metre changes as the group size halves. The count doubles. Explain that inverse relationship in words before writing equations.

Predict 1 ÷ 1/16 without drawing all sixteen pieces. Then explain what 1/2 ÷ 1/16 means and why it equals eight. A good generalisation lets you solve a new case while still being able to return to the physical model if challenged.
## Explain and transfer
Before every division, identify the starting amount, the group size or number of recipients, and what the quotient measures. Estimate whether the quotient should be greater or smaller than one. Then verify by multiplying quotient and divisor to recover the dividend.

For a final notebook task, invent a sharing story and a measuring-groups story that both use 3/4. Explain why one answer has a liquid-volume unit while the other may count cups. This is the understanding that makes the reciprocal rule reliable instead of fragile.
