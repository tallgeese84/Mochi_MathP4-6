# Angles and shape properties

## An angle measures a turn
An angle describes the turn between two rays meeting at a vertex. Its size does not depend on how long the drawn rays are. A right angle is 90°, a straight angle 180° and one full turn 360°. An acute angle is smaller than 90°; an obtuse angle lies between 90° and 180°.

In ∠ABC, the middle letter B names the vertex. When using a protractor, place its centre at the vertex and align its zero line with one ray. Choose the scale starting at that zero, rather than whichever printed number is easier to see.
## Combine known relationships
Angles forming a straight line total 180°. Angles around a point total 360°. When two straight lines cross, vertically opposite angles are equal. This follows because each is supplementary to the same adjacent angle: subtract that adjacent angle from 180°.

For adjacent angles of 65° and x on a straight line, x = 180° − 65° = 115°. State the straight-line relationship, not just the subtraction. In a complex diagram, a correct arithmetic operation needs a geometric reason to justify using those particular angles together.
## Triangles
The three interior angles of a triangle total 180°. A triangle with angles 48° and 67° therefore has a third angle of 65°. In an isosceles triangle, equal sides face equal angles. In an equilateral triangle all three sides and angles are equal, giving 60° each.

If an isosceles triangle's unequal angle is 40°, the other two share 140°, so each is 70°. Do not assume the labelled angle is the unequal one merely because it is drawn at the top. Use the equal-side marks or an explicit statement to identify the relevant pair.
## Quadrilaterals
A rectangle has four right angles and opposite sides equal and parallel. A square also has all four sides equal. A parallelogram has opposite sides parallel and equal, opposite angles equal, and adjacent interior angles summing to 180°. A rhombus is a parallelogram with all sides equal.

In Singapore school usage, a trapezium has one pair of opposite sides parallel. For this course, use the properties stated or marked in a question when classifying special cases. Do not infer equal sides or extra parallel sides solely from how a trapezium has been sketched.
## Worked deduction
A parallelogram has one interior angle of 72°. Its adjacent angle is 180° − 72° = 108°, and the opposite angles match these values. If a diagonal divides a known angle into two pieces, their sum still equals the original interior angle.

Suppose the 108° angle is split into 35° and x. Then x = 73°. This is a sequence of deductions: first identify the whole angle using the shape property, then subtract the known part. Write those reasons beside the calculations so another person can follow the chain.
## Diagnose and investigate
A pupil measures an angle from a screen drawing and gets 58°, while stated properties imply 60°. A schematic diagram may not be drawn to scale. For a deduction question, use given numbers and properties; measuring the picture is not a substitute for reasoning.

Cut a paper triangle and arrange its three corners along a straight line as an exploration of the angle sum. This physical demonstration helps you see the relationship, but imperfect cutting and measurement will not produce an exact proof. Distinguish an illustrative model from an exact logical argument.
## Explain and transfer
Draw a diagram with two intersecting lines and label one angle. Deduce the other three using both the straight-line sum and vertically opposite equality. Then create an isosceles triangle problem and mark the equal sides clearly.

Before practice, name the vertex, identify the relationship, calculate, and check whether the result fits all given constraints. If a deduction requires an unmarked right angle or a new line not justified by the problem, reconsider it. The drawing is a record of information, not permission to invent it.
