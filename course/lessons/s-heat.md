# Heat transfer and temperature

## Temperature is a measurement
Temperature tells us how hot or cold something is and is measured with a thermometer. Degrees Celsius are used in this course. Touch is not a reliable measuring instrument: two objects at the same temperature can feel different because they transfer heat at different rates.

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Do not use “heat” and “temperature” as interchangeable quantities. A large container and a small container of water can have the same temperature without containing the same total amount of thermal energy. No numerical heat-energy formula is needed in this primary lesson.
## Direction of transfer
Heat flows from a region at higher temperature to a region at lower temperature. A warm drink loses heat to cooler surroundings and its temperature generally falls. A cold drink gains heat from warmer surroundings and its temperature generally rises, provided no change of state or other complication dominates.

The cooler object does not send a substance called cold into the warmer one. Explain the direction using heat transfer. When two objects reach the same temperature, there is no net heat transfer between them due to a temperature difference.
## Conductors and insulators
Good conductors allow heat to pass through readily. Metals are generally good heat conductors compared with wood or plastic in common classroom examples. Poor conductors slow heat transfer and are often called heat insulators.

An insulator does not create heat or stop transfer perfectly. A jacket reduces heat loss from a warm body; an insulated bottle can slow warming of a cold drink as well as cooling of a hot drink. Explain the actual direction of heat transfer in each situation rather than memorising that insulation “keeps things hot”.
## Expansion and contraction
Many materials expand when heated and contract when cooled. Expansion means an increase in dimensions; it does not mean new matter has been added. Everyday designs may allow space for expansion when temperature changes.

These are general patterns, not a claim that every substance behaves identically at every temperature. In particular, water has special behaviour around freezing. For a primary question, use the stated material and conditions and avoid inventing precise expansion amounts without measurements.
## Worked example: two spoons
Two spoons of the same shape, one metal and one plastic, stand in the same warm water. Compare temperatures at corresponding points on their handles after equal times. The metal handle generally warms more quickly because metal conducts heat more readily.

Control dimensions, immersed length, starting temperature and water conditions for a fair comparison. Feeling the handle can suggest a difference, but a suitable temperature measurement gives stronger evidence. Do not use boiling water or touch very hot objects for this activity.
## Read a cooling record
Imagine a drink measured at 60°C, 48°C, 40°C and 35°C at equal time intervals in a 25°C room. The temperature falls, and the successive decreases become smaller. The evidence supports cooling toward room temperature under those conditions.

It does not show that the drink will fall below room temperature by that same heat-transfer process. Nor should you extend a straight line indefinitely below zero. Use the physical explanation as a check on graph extrapolation. Different containers and air movement can change the rate.
## Explain and transfer
For a heat question, identify the warmer and cooler objects, state the direction of transfer, identify whether the material speeds or slows it, then connect that to the observed temperature or state change.

In your notebook, compare an ice box and a winter coat. Both use insulation, but heat enters the cold box and leaves the warmer body. One general principle explains both. This is the kind of transfer that shows understanding beyond memorised examples.
