# Living things and classification

## What makes something alive?
Living things need resources, grow and develop, respond to their surroundings, and can reproduce as part of their life cycle. No single quick observation is sufficient in every case. A car moves and uses fuel but is not alive. A seed may appear inactive while still being alive.

Ask for a combination of characteristics. A particular animal may be unable to reproduce, but it remains a living thing. Dead leaves came from something living; a stone did not. These distinctions prevent a simple “moves or does not move” rule from producing misleading classifications.
## Group to make sense of diversity
Classification groups things by shared characteristics. We can sort objects by material, shape or use, but we must state the rule and apply it consistently. Scientific classification uses meaningful similarities among organisms, rather than personal preferences such as “nice animals”.

At primary level, recognise broad groups including plants, animals, fungi and bacteria. Flowering and non-flowering plants are plant groups. Fungi are not plants: they do not make food by photosynthesis. Bacteria are living organisms too small to see individually with the unaided eye. Do not grow unknown microbes at home to investigate them.
## Compare plant groups
Flowering plants produce flowers as part of reproduction, although flowers may be small or appear only during some seasons. Many produce fruits containing seeds. Non-flowering plants do not produce flowers; ferns reproduce by spores, while some other non-flowering plants produce seeds.

Therefore “all non-flowering plants reproduce by spores” is too broad. A single photograph with no visible flower also does not prove that a plant is non-flowering. Reliable classification may require observations across its life cycle or a trustworthy reference about that organism.
## Recognise animal groups
Useful primary animal groups include mammals, birds, fish, reptiles, amphibians and insects. Mammals have hair and females feed their young with milk. Birds have feathers. Fish typically have fins and gills. Insects have six legs as adults and three main body parts.

Use several relevant characteristics when comparing reptiles and amphibians: their skin, reproduction and life cycles differ. Do not rely only on habitat. Whales live in water but are mammals; penguins are birds even though they do not fly. Exceptions help us improve an over-simple rule rather than abandon careful observation.
## Worked example: an identification key
Imagine three animals: a butterfly, a bird and a cat. Start with a feature that separates them: “Does it have feathers?” separates the bird. For the remaining two, “Does the adult have six legs?” identifies the butterfly. The cat follows the other branch.

Every organism in the set must reach one unambiguous endpoint. A key is a tool for a defined collection; it is not proof that the chosen feature classifies every animal on Earth. If you add a spider, the key needs another distinguishing question because a spider is neither an insect nor a mammal.
## Observe without harming
Study the living-world illustration as a scene to explore, not a complete species guide. List visible organisms, their observable features and one fact that would require a reference. You cannot reliably infer every animal’s diet from a picture.

On a walk, photograph leaves, bark and insects without touching unfamiliar organisms. Build a two-choice key for a small set of leaves using features such as edge shape and arrangement. Check the key with someone who has not seen your notes. If two leaves reach the same endpoint, refine your questions.
## Explain and transfer
A good classification answer names the relevant feature and links it to the group. “It is a bird because it has feathers” is more informative than “it looks like a bird”. Do not claim that all flying animals are birds or all water animals are fish.

When a new example breaks your rule, ask whether the rule was necessary, sufficient or merely common. In everyday language: must every member have that feature, and does having that feature guarantee membership? This is the same careful reasoning used in mathematical definitions.
