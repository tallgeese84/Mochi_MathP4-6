# Ratios and equal units

## A relationship between quantities
A mixture contains two cups of concentrate for every three cups of water. Its ratio of concentrate to water is 2 : 3. The order matters: water to concentrate is 3 : 2. The ratio describes relative amounts, not necessarily the actual number of cups in a particular jug.

Four cups of concentrate and six cups of water have the same ratio. Each part has been multiplied by two. Adding the same amount to both quantities does not generally preserve the ratio: 2 : 3 and 3 : 4 describe different mixtures. Use matching units before writing a ratio.
## Draw ratio units
Represent 2 : 3 by two equal boxes for one quantity and three equal boxes for the other. The total contains five boxes. Therefore concentrate is 2/5 of the whole mixture, not 2/3 of it. The fraction 2/3 compares concentrate with water only.

If the mixture totals 25 cups, one box represents 25 ÷ 5 = 5 cups. Concentrate is 10 cups and water is 15 cups. The boxes have equal values within this model. They are not physical cups until we calculate what each box represents.
## Work from a difference
Ali and Bea have beads in the ratio 3 : 5. Bea has 14 more beads than Ali. The difference of two ratio units corresponds to 14 beads, so one unit is seven. Ali has 21 beads and Bea has 35. Their total is 56.

A common mistake divides 14 by eight because eight is the number of total units. But 14 describes the difference, not the total. Before using any given number, draw an arrow showing which part of the model it labels. This single step resolves many ratio difficulties.
## Three-part sharing
Share $72 among three people in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4. The total is nine units, each worth $8. The shares are $16, $24 and $32. Add them to check $72, then divide by their common factor eight to check the original ratio.

To simplify 12 : 18 : 24, divide all terms by the same common factor six, giving 2 : 3 : 4. Simplifying only two terms would alter the three-way relationship. The current primary core uses whole-number ratio terms; more elaborate ratio notation belongs to later enrichment.
## A changing collection
Initially red and blue beads are in the ratio 2 : 3. Only red beads are added, and the ratio becomes 4 : 3. Since the blue count is unchanged and its ratio number is already three in both descriptions, the same unit size works. The added red beads represent two original units.

If the stated ratios were 2 : 3 and 5 : 6, first rename the initial ratio as 4 : 6. Now six units of blue match in both states, making the red increase one common unit. 'One ratio unit' has no fixed value across separately drawn models until a relationship establishes it.
## Guided task
A recipe uses flour, sugar and butter in the ratio 5 : 2 : 3 by mass. If butter is 180 g, three units are 180 g, so one unit is 60 g. Flour is 300 g and sugar is 120 g. Total mass is 600 g.

Ask whether doubling only the flour keeps the recipe's proportions. It does not. Doubling every ingredient does. Explain the distinction using equivalent ratios, then describe why adding 60 g to every ingredient changes the relative composition even though the additions are equal.
## Explain and transfer
When you read a ratio, identify the order, the units and whether the given amount labels one part, a total or a difference. If quantities change, search for what stays unchanged: a count, a total or a difference. Scale models to that common anchor.

Before practice, explain why a 2 : 3 ratio gives a two-fifths share of the total. Use both boxes and actual objects. Then create a problem where the same ratio comes with a known difference instead of a known total, and solve it without changing the meaning of a box.
