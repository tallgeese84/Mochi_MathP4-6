# Fractions when the whole changes

## The word remaining changes the model
Euna spends one quarter of her money, then one third of the remaining money. The second fraction refers to a smaller whole. We cannot simply add 1/4 and 1/3 of the original amount. Draw one bar for the original amount, shade the first quarter, and label the unshaded three quarters 'new reference whole'.

The phrase 'one third of the remainder' means partitioning that unshaded portion into three equal parts. Each of those parts is one quarter of the original bar. This picture reveals the relationship before any original dollar amount is known.
## Follow the amount forwards
Suppose the original amount is $80. After spending a quarter, Euna has 3/4 × $80 = $60. She next spends one third of $60, which is $20, leaving $40. The final remainder is one half of the original amount.

We can express the same reasoning without dollars: remaining fraction = 3/4 × 2/3 = 1/2. The factors describe what survives each stage. Multiplying the spent fractions instead would describe a different quantity. Label 'left' and 'spent' explicitly in your notebook.
## Work backwards from the end
Now suppose only the final $40 is given. It represents two thirds of the amount before the second purchase. One third is $20, so that earlier amount is $60. This $60 represents three quarters of the original. One quarter is $20, so the original was $80.

Working backwards reverses each relationship in reverse order. It is not enough to reverse operations while keeping their original order. Draw arrows between 'original', 'after first purchase' and 'after second purchase', and write a fraction of the earlier amount on each arrow.
## A different kind of second step
A bottle starts full. One quarter of the water is poured out, then another 120 ml is poured out. There are 330 ml left. Here the second removal is a fixed volume, not a fraction of the remainder. Add back 120 ml first to recover 450 ml after the first removal.

That 450 ml is three quarters of the full bottle. One quarter is 150 ml, giving an original volume of 600 ml. Check forwards: 600 − 150 − 120 = 330. Similar-looking wording can encode different relationships; identify the quantities rather than matching the question to a memorised template.
## Spot the hidden assumption
A pupil says spending half your money twice leaves nothing. That is true if both halves mean half of the original amount. It is false if the second half means half of what remains: first one half remains, then half of that remains, leaving one quarter of the original.

Ask the pupil to write two precise stories and solve each with $40. One ends at $0 and the other at $10. The disagreement is about the reference whole, not about arithmetic. Clear labels can resolve an apparently difficult problem before calculating.
## Guided task with a diagram
A class lends one third of its books, then one quarter of the books still present. It ends with 30 books. The last 30 are three quarters of the intermediate amount, so the intermediate amount is 40. That 40 is two thirds of the original, so the original is 60.

Draw an original bar in twelve equal parts. Removing one third takes four parts. Removing one quarter of the remaining eight takes two more, leaving six of twelve. Explain why this confirms that the final 30 books represent half of the original collection.
## Explain and transfer
Create a two-stage story using a fraction followed by a fixed amount. Give only the final remainder to another learner. Ask them to identify the two reference quantities before solving. Keep your own forward calculation as an independent check of their answer.

Before practice, use this checklist: name each whole, identify whether each change is fractional or fixed, show one intermediate amount, and check the final result forwards. The method should work for money, water, ribbon or books because the underlying relationship is the same.
