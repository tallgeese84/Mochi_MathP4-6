# Volume, layers and conserved water

## Count space in three dimensions
A unit cube has edges one unit long and volume one cubic unit. A cuboid five cubes long and three cubes across contains fifteen cubes in one layer. Four identical layers contain sixty cubes, explaining volume = length × breadth × height.

Area counts two-dimensional coverage; volume counts three-dimensional occupied space. Their units must differ. A cuboid with edges measured in centimetres has volume in cubic centimetres, written cm³. A metre cube is much larger than a centimetre cube; conversion between cm³ and m³ is outside the core focus here.
## Base area times height
The product of length and breadth is the base area. Therefore volume = base area × perpendicular height. A tank with base area 120 cm² and water depth 8 cm contains 960 cm³ of water. Use water depth, not the full height of the empty space above it.

The same relationship can recover an unknown: height = volume ÷ base area. If 1,200 cm³ fills a tank with base area 150 cm², the depth is 8 cm. Explain the division as the number of equal one-centimetre layers needed to hold the water.
## Capacity and liquid volume
One millilitre corresponds to one cubic centimetre, so 1,000 cm³ = 1,000 ml = 1 litre. A cuboid tank measuring 20 cm by 15 cm by 10 cm internally has capacity 3,000 cm³, or 3 litres, when completely filled.

A half-full tank of those dimensions contains 1.5 litres. The tank's capacity has not changed; its current contents have. In real measurement, wall thickness matters when outside dimensions are provided, so use internal dimensions or the assumptions stated by the problem.
## Worked transfer problem
Tank A contains water to depth 12 cm over a 10 cm by 8 cm base. Its water volume is 960 cm³. All of it is poured into Tank B, whose base is 16 cm by 10 cm. Assuming no loss or overflow, B's depth is 960 ÷ 160 = 6 cm.

The wider base spreads the same volume into fewer layers. The heights need not match because height is not the conserved quantity. State the conditions: all water transferred, no leakage, no evaporation of consequence and sufficient receiving capacity. These assumptions justify equating the volumes.
## Recover a cube edge
A cube has volume 125 cm³. Its edge length is the number that multiplies by itself three times to give 125. Since 5 × 5 × 5 = 125, the edge is 5 cm. Dividing the volume by three would not reverse cubing.

For a cuboid of volume 240 cm³ and height 6 cm, the base area is 40 cm². That does not uniquely determine its length and breadth: 8 by 5 and 10 by 4 both work. Distinguish what the information determines from what remains unknown.
## Guided investigation
Build two cuboids using the same twelve unit cubes. One may be 1 by 3 by 4 and another 2 by 2 by 3. Their volumes agree even though their shapes and exposed surface areas differ. Draw the layers you counted, including cubes hidden from view.

Pour equal measured amounts of room-temperature water into a narrow and a wide container. Predict which water level will be higher before checking. If the containers do not have constant rectangular cross-sections, the simple cuboid formula may not apply; measurement can still establish equal volume.
## Explain and transfer
When solving a tank problem, label the base, the water depth and the tank's maximum height separately. Convert litres to cubic centimetres when needed before dividing by a base area measured in cm². Check whether the calculated depth fits inside the tank.

Before practice, explain why doubling a cuboid's height alone doubles its volume, while doubling all three dimensions multiplies volume by eight. The explanation should refer to layers and cubes, connecting the formula back to a physical model.
