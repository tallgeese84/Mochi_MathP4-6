# Materials: choose properties for a purpose

## Object and material
An object is something made or used; a material is what it is made from. A spoon might be made of metal, wood or plastic. One material can have many uses, and one object can contain several materials. A raincoat has fabric, fasteners and possibly a waterproof coating.

When comparing materials, name the property needed for the job. Appearance alone rarely explains suitability. A transparent window must allow enough light through; a safe handle should provide a comfortable grip and suit the temperatures involved.
## Strength and flexibility
Strength concerns how well a material withstands a load or force without breaking under a specified test. Flexibility concerns how readily it bends. They are different: something flexible is not necessarily weak, and something stiff is not necessarily strong against every kind of force.

Test equal-sized samples using the same arrangement when you want to compare materials. A thick strip and a thin strip may behave differently because of their dimensions, even if their materials are identical. Describe the test: resistance to pulling, bending or impact can lead to different results.
## Water and light
A waterproof material prevents water passing through under the tested conditions. An absorbent material takes water in. A towel is useful because it absorbs water; a raincoat’s outer layer should resist water passing through. These are different design goals, not a ranking from good to bad.

Materials also differ in how much light passes through. Clear glass allows much light through; some frosted materials allow light through but prevent a clear view; cardboard blocks it. The words transparent, translucent and opaque are useful vocabulary, but the important scientific idea is the observed passage of light.
## Floating is an observation
Some objects float in water and others sink. Test safely with clean water and small non-sharp objects. Record both the material and the object’s form. A hollow metal boat and a solid metal piece can behave differently; “all metal sinks” is therefore not a reliable rule for every object.

A detailed explanation using average density is extension work, not a required calculation in this primary unit. Here, describe observations accurately and avoid turning a result from one sample into a claim about every possible shape or size made from that material.
## Worked design: a lunch carrier
Suppose a carrier must withstand light rain, hold a lunch box and bend enough to fold away. Consider waterproofness, strength and flexibility. Test equal-sized samples, then explain a choice using the observations. Colour may matter to the user, but it does not establish waterproofness.

There may be a trade-off: a very strong rigid container may be less convenient to fold. Combining materials can meet different needs, such as a strong outer fabric with a waterproof lining. A design answer should connect each required function to a supporting property.
## Try and record
With an adult, place equal-sized samples of paper towel, plastic sheet and cloth over separate cups. Add the same small amount of room-temperature water and observe after the same time. Protect the table and keep water away from electrical items.

Write what happened before explaining it. Water may soak into a sample without immediately dripping through, so record both absorption and leakage. This distinction improves your conclusion. Repeat with fresh samples if an earlier sample is already wet.
## Explain and transfer
Use the chain requirement, property, evidence and decision. “Choose this material because it is good” skips the reasoning. “The outer layer must keep rain out; this sample prevented water passing through in our test” makes the connection visible.

An investigation supports a conclusion under tested conditions. Very high pressure or a long exposure may produce a different result. This is not a reason to avoid conclusions; it is a reason to state their limits clearly.
