# Measurement, units and sensible precision

## What property is being measured?
Length measures how far along something extends. Mass measures the amount of matter using units such as grams and kilograms. Volume measures occupied space; capacity describes how much a container can hold. These quantities are related in everyday tasks but are not interchangeable.

A litre is a volume unit, not a mass unit. Two liquids with equal volumes need not have equal masses. In mathematics, use the conversion or physical condition given by the problem; do not assume that one millilitre always has a mass of one gram for every substance.
## Units are repeatable references
One metre is 100 centimetres, one kilometre is 1,000 metres, one kilogram is 1,000 grams and one litre is 1,000 millilitres. When converting to smaller units, more units are needed to describe the same quantity. Thus 2.35 m becomes 235 cm.

When converting 750 ml to litres, divide by 1,000 to obtain 0.75 litre. The liquid amount has not changed; only the name of the unit and the number used to count it have changed. This is a form of equivalence, not a physical transformation.
## Read a scale carefully
A measuring jug may label 100 ml and 200 ml with four equally spaced marks between them. Those marks divide the gap into five intervals, so each interval is 20 ml. Count spaces between marks rather than only the printed marks themselves.

Read a ruler from zero when possible. If an object begins at 2 cm and ends at 9.5 cm, its length is 9.5 − 2 = 7.5 cm. Reporting 9.5 cm would confuse the ending position with the distance between endpoints. Sketch both endpoints before subtracting.
## Worked comparison
A parcel has mass 1.8 kg and another has mass 1,950 g. Convert to a common unit: 1.8 kg = 1,800 g. The second parcel is heavier by 150 g. Alternatively convert 1,950 g to 1.95 kg and find a difference of 0.15 kg.

Check that both forms of the difference agree. Writing 1.8 − 1,950 without matching units mixes quantities expressed on different scales. Units should stay attached throughout the calculation, particularly when a problem includes several kinds of measurement.
## Precision and estimation
A ruler marked in centimetres cannot support a confident measurement to one thousandth of a centimetre. Record a sensible precision for the apparatus. In a word problem, follow the requested accuracy; in a real measurement, acknowledge uncertainty rather than inventing extra digits.

Estimate first. The length of a classroom is more plausibly several metres than several millimetres. A child's mass is more plausibly tens of kilograms than tens of grams. These comparisons catch unit errors that an otherwise correct multiplication algorithm might not reveal.
## Guided practical task
Choose three safe household objects and estimate their lengths. Measure them with a ruler, record the units and compare with your estimates. If your ruler is shorter than an object, measure successive lengths without leaving gaps or counting the same section twice.

Pour room-temperature water between two containers of different shapes with an adult nearby if needed. A higher water level does not necessarily mean more water: the container's cross-sectional shape also matters. Use a measuring jug to compare volumes instead of judging from height alone.
## Explain and transfer
Create a conversion chain for 3.06 kg: 3 kg + 60 g = 3,060 g. Explain why the decimal part is sixty grams rather than six grams. Then create a similar chain for metres and centimetres, noticing that their conversion factor is one hundred rather than one thousand.

Before practice, identify the measured property, choose a common unit and predict whether the numerical value should increase or decrease after conversion. Finally check the answer against a familiar object of similar size. Measurement connects abstract calculation to the physical world.
