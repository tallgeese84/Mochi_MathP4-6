# Investigations: explain why a claim holds

## Examples suggest; reasons establish
The sums 1 + 3 = 4, 3 + 5 = 8 and 7 + 9 = 16 suggest that two odd numbers sum to an even number. Examples help us notice the pattern, but a finite list alone does not establish it for every pair of odd numbers.

Represent an odd number as pairs of counters plus one unpaired counter. Combining two odd collections brings together all the pairs and the two unpaired counters, which form one additional pair. Nothing remains unpaired, so the sum is even. This explains the entire family at once.
## One counterexample can defeat always
Consider the claim 'Every number ending in 3 is prime'. Thirteen and twenty-three support it, but 33 = 3 × 11 defeats it. One valid counterexample is enough because the word every includes 33. Giving more supporting examples cannot repair the original universal claim.

A counterexample must satisfy the claim's starting conditions while failing its conclusion. The number 35 is composite, but it does not end in 3 and therefore is not a counterexample to this particular statement. State which condition your example meets and which conclusion it contradicts.
## Count systematically
How many outfits can be made from three shirts and two pairs of trousers? Draw a table with shirts as rows and trousers as columns. Each of its six cells represents a different pairing. The table explains both completeness and uniqueness: every shirt has both options, and no cell repeats a pair.

If additional restrictions are given, cross out the forbidden cells rather than still using the unrestricted product. Counting methods must reflect the actual rules. Distinguish ordered arrangements from unordered selections; red-then-blue may differ from blue-then-red in a sequence but not in a bag of two counters.
## Look for something that cannot change
An invariant is a property preserved by an allowed operation. If you add two to a whole number repeatedly, its odd-or-even status never changes. Therefore starting from an odd number, you cannot reach an even number using only that operation.

This is stronger than trying many unsuccessful sequences. It explains why no permitted sequence can succeed. In a new puzzle, inspect totals, parity, colour balance or remainder after division. The useful invariant depends on the operation; do not assume the same one fits every problem.
## A worst-case guarantee
A bag contains red and blue counters, with at least three of each. Draw without looking. Two draws might give one of each colour, so two do not guarantee a matching pair. With three draws, at least two must share a colour because there are only two colour categories.

The argument concerns a guarantee, not the most likely outcome. Three may be needed even though a matching pair often occurs in two. To justify a minimum, give a possible arrangement showing that one fewer can fail, then explain why the proposed number must succeed.
## Optimise with a complete comparison
Rectangles with whole-number side lengths and area 24 have side pairs 1 by 24, 2 by 12, 3 by 8 and 4 by 6. Their perimeters are 50, 28, 22 and 20. Thus 4 by 6 has the smallest perimeter among these permitted rectangles.

The restriction to whole-number sides matters. Without it, more rectangles are possible and this case list is no longer complete. A valid optimum must be best among all allowed choices, not merely the few examples tried. Explain why your search covers the permitted set.
## Explain, challenge and read further
Present an investigation with a conjecture, a strategy, evidence and a conclusion. Invite someone to challenge it. If their example breaks your claim, revise the claim precisely rather than treating the challenge as failure. Mathematical creativity and careful criticism work together.
