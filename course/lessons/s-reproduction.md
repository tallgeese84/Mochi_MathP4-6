# Reproduction in plants and humans

## Cells and new organisms
Living things are made of cells, the basic units of life. In sexual reproduction, a male reproductive cell joins with a female reproductive cell during fertilisation. The resulting fertilised cell can develop into a new organism under suitable conditions.

Reproduction enables a kind of organism to continue across generations. It is different from growth of an existing individual. This lesson uses accurate, age-appropriate biological terms. A parent or teacher can discuss questions that arise; detailed human reproductive anatomy beyond the primary syllabus is not required here.
## Pollination in flowering plants
Pollen is produced in the anthers. Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. Wind or animals such as insects can help transfer pollen, depending on the plant. The stigma and anther have different roles; pollination is not the movement of an entire seed.

Some flowers have features that help attract pollinators, while wind-pollinated flowers may look different. Do not infer the pollination method solely from one attractive illustration. Use the flower’s features and reliable information about the plant.
## Fertilisation and seed formation
After suitable pollination, a male reproductive cell can fuse with a female reproductive cell during fertilisation. Fertilisation is distinct from pollen transfer. In the usual primary flowering-plant model, fertilised ovules develop into seeds and the ovary develops into the fruit.

A pollinated flower does not automatically guarantee successful seed formation: fertilisation and suitable development must follow. The important sequence is pollination, fertilisation, then seed and fruit development. Detailed pollen-tube growth is beyond what this primary lesson requires.
## Seed dispersal and germination
Dispersal moves seeds away from the parent plant. Wind, water, animals or splitting fruits may help, depending on the seed or fruit’s structure. Wings, hairs, buoyant coverings, hooks or edible fruits can be connected to suitable dispersal methods.

Dispersal can reduce competition near the parent and help plants reach new places. Germination is the start of growth of a seed into a seedling under suitable conditions, including water, oxygen and a suitable temperature. Light requirements differ among seeds; do not assume all seeds require light to begin germination.
## Human reproduction
In humans, testes produce sperm cells and ovaries produce egg cells. Fertilisation occurs when a sperm cell fuses with an egg cell. The fertilised egg can develop into a baby in the womb, also called the uterus.

The baby does not develop in the stomach, which belongs to the digestive system. Names of body parts help distinguish their functions. Detailed stages of fetal development and the umbilical cord are beyond the required primary scope of this lesson.
## Worked comparison
Compare a flowering plant and a human. Both use male and female reproductive cells in sexual reproduction, and fertilisation begins development of a new individual. Their organs, methods of bringing cells together and later development differ.

Now compare pollination and seed dispersal. Pollination moves pollen before fertilisation; dispersal moves seeds after they have formed. Confusing these processes changes the sequence and explanation. Build a timeline and state what is moving at each stage.
## Explain and transfer
Choose an unfamiliar seed image from a reliable reference. Describe an observed feature, propose a dispersal method, then explain how the feature helps. A wing-like structure may help wind transport; a hook may attach to an animal. The appearance suggests a hypothesis, which observations can test.

For human biology, practise naming the reproductive cells, their producing organs and the place where the developing baby grows. Use respectful scientific language and ask an adult about anything unclear. The goal is accurate understanding of life processes, not memorising an oversized anatomy list.
