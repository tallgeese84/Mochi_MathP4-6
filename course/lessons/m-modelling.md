# Turn a word problem into a model

## Read for relationships
A word problem contains quantities and relationships. First say what is unknown in a complete sentence. Then list what is known, including units. Some information describes a total, some a difference, some a repeated group and some a change. A useful model records those relationships without yet deciding every calculation.

Do not choose operations from single keywords. 'More' may describe a comparison where the smaller amount must be found by subtraction. 'Altogether' may describe a known total from which a missing part is removed. Explain the situation in your own words before using symbols.
## Sum and difference
Two lengths total 58 cm, and the longer exceeds the shorter by 14 cm. Draw two bars aligned at one end. Removing the excess 14 cm from the total leaves two equal copies of the shorter length: 58 − 14 = 44 cm. Each copy is 22 cm, and the longer is 36 cm.

Verify both conditions: 22 + 36 = 58 and 36 − 22 = 14. Checking only the sum could allow many wrong pairs. Every condition in the original story should be used either to construct the solution or to test it.
## Work backwards
A number is multiplied by three and then increased by eight to give 41. Reverse the last change first: 41 − 8 = 33. Then undo the multiplication: 33 ÷ 3 = 11. Check forwards with 11 × 3 + 8 = 41.

A bar model and an equation, 3x + 8 = 41, describe the same structure. Choose the representation that helps you see the inverse steps. Working backwards is particularly useful when the final amount is known and each preceding transformation is reversible.
## Make an organised table
Tickets cost $3 for a child and $5 for an adult. Eight tickets cost $32. Begin with eight child tickets costing $24. Each replacement of a child ticket by an adult ticket adds $2. We need $8 more, so four replacements are needed: four adults and four children.

An alternative table lists adult counts from zero to eight and the corresponding totals. The total rises by $2 each step because the number of tickets remains fixed. Explain why the list is exhaustive and ordered; random guesses would make it harder to know when to stop.
## Follow a multi-part dependency
Suppose part (a) finds the water left in a tank and part (b) asks how many bottles it fills. Label the result of part (a) with its meaning and unit. Reuse it as an input to part (b), converting units if needed. A correct early calculation should not become an unexplained number later.

If a later result is impossible, trace the dependency backwards. Did you use full tank capacity instead of remaining water? Did you count partial bottles as complete ones? A structured model helps locate which assumption or step needs repair.
## A useful failed attempt
A model can fail productively. If you assume all eight tickets are adult tickets, the cost is $40, too high by $8. Replacing an adult by a child reduces the total by $2, again giving four replacements. Two different starting assumptions converge on the same result.

Keep a short note about why your first approach stalled. Perhaps a ratio unit was not fixed, a difference was mistaken for a total, or a case list was incomplete. Such notes help you select a representation next time without assigning yourself a permanent 'learning style'.
## Explain and transfer
Write a solution with four parts: model, calculation, interpretation and check. Ask someone to cover your calculations and judge whether the model alone reflects the story. Then let them examine the arithmetic separately. This separates a conceptual error from a computational slip.

For transfer, change the context while preserving the relationship: ticket prices can become two kinds of coins or packages. Then change one relationship while preserving the context. Recognising both sameness and difference is more powerful than memorising named question types.
