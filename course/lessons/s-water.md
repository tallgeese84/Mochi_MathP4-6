# Water changes state and cycles

## One substance, different states
Ice is water in the solid state. Liquid water is water in the liquid state. Water vapour is water in the gaseous state. Melting changes ice into liquid water when it gains heat; the substance is still water. This is a change of state, not the production of a different substance.

During the melting of pure ice under normal conditions, heat can be gained while the temperature stays about 0°C until melting is complete. Heat gain does not always mean an immediate temperature rise.

Freezing changes liquid water into ice when it loses heat. Under normal atmospheric conditions, pure water freezes and ice melts at about 0°C. Impurities and pressure can affect these temperatures, so keep the usual classroom conditions in mind.
## Evaporation and boiling
Evaporation is the change from liquid water into water vapour at the surface. It can occur below the boiling point. Boiling occurs throughout the liquid, with bubbles of water vapour forming. Pure water boils at about 100°C under normal atmospheric pressure.

Visible white mist above hot water consists mainly of tiny liquid droplets, not invisible water vapour itself. Do not describe every visible cloud as a gas. The distinction matters when explaining condensation on a mirror or the formation of clouds.
## What affects evaporation?
For comparable conditions, a higher water temperature, a larger exposed surface area and greater air movement can increase the rate of evaporation. A spread-out wet cloth commonly dries faster than the same cloth folded up because more wet surface is exposed.

Humidity also affects evaporation, but treat it as useful context rather than an additional memorised primary formula. In an investigation, keep other conditions the same. If a sunny location is also windier, the result does not isolate temperature alone.
## Condensation
Condensation changes water vapour into liquid water when it loses heat. Water vapour in surrounding air can lose heat near a cold glass, forming droplets on the outside. Those droplets need not have leaked through the glass.

A lid held above warm water can also collect droplets as water vapour cools. Never try this over boiling water without an appropriate adult-led demonstration. Explain where the water vapour came from and what it transferred heat to, rather than writing only “cold makes water”.
## Worked explanation: a cold bottle
A sealed dry bottle containing cold water is placed in a warm room. Droplets appear outside. The cold surface cools nearby water vapour sufficiently for condensation. The external liquid can therefore originate from water vapour in the room air.

A useful comparison is a similar sealed bottle at room temperature. Keep location and observation time the same. The comparison helps investigate the effect of surface temperature. It still does not measure the amount of water vapour directly, so avoid claiming more than the observations show.
## The water cycle
Energy from the Sun drives evaporation from water surfaces and other sources. Water vapour can cool and condense into tiny droplets that form clouds. Water returns to the ground as precipitation such as rain; some collects in water bodies, flows over land or enters the ground.

## Explain and transfer
For every state change, name the starting state, ending state and heat gain or loss. “Water disappears” fails to explain evaporation; “ice melts into liquid water as it gains heat” identifies the process clearly.

Try connecting three everyday cases: drying laundry, a fogged mirror and ice in a drink. They involve evaporation, condensation and melting respectively. Compare their heat transfers and identify the source of the water in each case before attempting independent questions.
