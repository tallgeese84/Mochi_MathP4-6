# Decimals are fractions in place-value clothing

## Extend the place-value chart
Place a decimal point after the ones column. To its right are tenths, hundredths and thousandths. Each step right divides the unit size by ten. Thus 0.406 means four tenths, no hundredths and six thousandths: 406/1000. The zero still protects a place, just as in whole numbers.

A decimal with more digits is not necessarily larger. Compare 0.8 and 0.75 by writing 0.80 and 0.75. Eight tenths are greater than seven tenths, so 0.8 is greater. Adding a zero at the far right of a decimal does not change its value; adding one between existing digits usually does.
## Add and subtract matching units
For 3.4 + 0.27, write 3.40 + 0.27 and align the place values. The result is 3.67. Aligning decimal points is useful because it aligns ones with ones and hundredths with hundredths. It is not an arbitrary writing convention.

For 5 − 2.38, write 5.00 and regroup as needed. The difference is 2.62. Verify by 2.38 + 2.62 = 5.00. Money can make hundredths familiar, but decimals also describe length, mass, temperature and other quantities that may need more than two decimal places.
## Multiply and divide with meaning
To calculate 0.24 × 3, regard 0.24 as 24 hundredths. Three copies are 72 hundredths, or 0.72. For 0.24 ÷ 3, share 24 hundredths into three groups to get eight hundredths, or 0.08. The place value explains where the digits belong.

Multiplying by 100 changes 0.247 into 24.7 because each digit gains a value one hundred times as large. Dividing 24.7 by 100 reverses that scaling. A description about 'moving the decimal point' can help record the calculation, but cannot replace the explanation of which quantity changes.
## Worked example: a measurement
A ribbon is 2.4 m long and is cut into six equal pieces. Each piece measures 2.4 ÷ 6 = 0.4 m. Convert to centimetres by noting that one metre contains 100 centimetres: 0.4 m = 40 cm. Check that six pieces of 40 cm total 240 cm, or 2.4 m.

A different ribbon measures 2.43 m. Dividing by six gives 0.405 m, or 40.5 cm. Do not round halfway through unless instructed. A premature approximation can produce a total that no longer matches the original length.
## Rounding and the boundary
Round 7.468 to two decimal places. The neighbouring hundredths are 7.46 and 7.47; the midpoint is 7.465. The number lies above that midpoint, so round to 7.47. Keep enough digits to decide the requested rounding once, from the original number.

For example, 1.449 rounded directly to one decimal place is 1.4. Rounding first to two decimal places gives 1.45, which would then round to 1.5. This is why repeated rounding can change the answer. Use the original measurement when reporting the requested precision.
## Guided comparison
Arrange 0.507, 0.57 and 0.075 in increasing order. Rename them as thousandths: 507, 570 and 75 thousandths. The order is 0.075, 0.507, 0.57. Explain the first place where each comparison is decided.

Next turn 3/8 into a decimal by division, obtaining 0.375. Reverse the representation: 0.375 = 375/1000 = 3/8. Converting in both directions checks that the two notations describe the same number. Some fractions have non-terminating decimals, so a rounded decimal may only be approximate.
## Explain and transfer
Find a supermarket mass label or a measuring jug scale. Read the unit, identify what one small division represents and estimate where a quantity between marks would fall. The scale, not the number of printed digits, determines what you can measure confidently.

Before practice, explain why 0.6 = 0.60, why 0.06 is different, and why 0.6 × 10 = 6. If these statements make sense in tenths and hundredths, decimal algorithms become easier to reconstruct when you forget a rule.
