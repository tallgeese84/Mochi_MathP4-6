# Light, sight and shadows

## Light helps us see
We see an object when light from it enters our eyes. A lamp and the Sun produce light. A book usually does not; we see it because light reflects from its surface into our eyes. In a completely dark room, merely opening our eyes does not make the book visible.

Distinguish a light source from an object that reflects light. The Moon reflects sunlight; it is not producing the visible light by the same process as the Sun. Never look directly at the Sun or shine bright light into anyone’s eyes.
## Straight paths
In a uniform medium, light travels in straight lines. A simple classroom model represents paths with straight lines and arrowheads indicating the direction of travel. A drawn line is a representation, not a visible string coming out of the source.

When a source, an opening and an observer are aligned, light can pass through the opening to the observer. Move an opaque screen into the path and the light is blocked. This straight-line model helps explain shadows and why we cannot see through a cardboard wall.
## Materials and transmission
Some materials allow much light through; some allow some light through but prevent a clear image; others block most light. The same material may behave differently at different thicknesses. Describe the observation under stated conditions rather than assuming all plastic behaves identically.

Reflection and transmission are different. A surface can reflect some light and transmit some light. For primary questions, focus on the property relevant to the setup: whether light reaches the screen or the eye, and whether an opaque object interrupts the path.
## Forming a shadow
A shadow forms where an object blocks light from reaching a surface. Identify the light source, object and screen. Without a receiving surface, a familiar shadow patch may not be visible even though light is blocked in a region.

For a small source and fixed source-to-screen distance, moving the object nearer the source generally makes its shadow larger. Moving it nearer the screen generally makes it smaller. This statement requires the stated geometry; do not apply it automatically when several distances change together.
## Worked explanation
Imagine a torch, a card and a wall along a line. Light passing around the edges of the card continues toward the wall. The card blocks paths through its area, leaving a darker region. Sketch two limiting straight paths past opposite card edges to reason about the shadow size.

Use arrowheads away from the source, and place labels outside the paths. A decorative illustration is not accurate evidence of angles or distances. For a geometry-based answer, use the interactive model or your measured setup and state which distances stayed fixed.
## Investigate safely
Use a small torch, a plain card and a wall in a safely dimmed room. Keep the torch and wall fixed. Move the card to measured positions and record shadow width. Keep card size and orientation the same. Avoid lasers, hot lamps and complete darkness that creates a tripping risk.

Predict before measuring. Repeat readings at the same positions and compare results. If the torch has a broad source, the shadow edges may be blurred; record that limitation rather than pretending every boundary is perfectly sharp.
## Explain and transfer
For sight, trace source to object to eye. For a shadow, trace source toward screen and identify which paths the object blocks. These two sequences answer different questions and should not be mixed.

Explain why a white wall is visible without producing its own light, then explain why a shadow moves when the source moves. A strong answer connects the light path to the observation instead of using “because light” as a complete explanation.
