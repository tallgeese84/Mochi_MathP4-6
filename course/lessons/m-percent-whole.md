# Reverse percentages and changing bases

## The given amount may be only a part
If 20% of a group is 14 pupils, the group is not 14 pupils. Fourteen corresponds to one of five equal 20% portions. Therefore the whole contains 14 × 5 = 70 pupils. Draw five equal boxes and label one box 14 to make the correspondence explicit.

The general relationship is part = percentage fraction × whole. To recover the whole, divide the part by that fraction. For 14 ÷ 0.2, the quotient 70 is larger than 14 because the known part is less than the whole. Predicting that direction provides an immediate check.
## Worked example: reverse discount
A jacket sells for $72 after a 20% discount. The sale price represents 80% of the original, not 20%. Eighty percent is four fifths, so one fifth is $18 and five fifths are $90. The original price was $90 and the discount was $18.

Check forwards: 20% of $90 is $18, and $90 − $18 = $72. Adding 20% of $72 would give $86.40, which fails this check. The percentage base for the original discount was $90, not the later sale price.
## Percentage increase and decrease
A plant grows from 40 cm to 50 cm. The increase is 10 cm. As a fraction of the original height, that is 10/40 = 1/4, or 25%. The calculation describes how large the change is compared with the starting amount.

If the height instead falls from 50 cm to 40 cm, the decrease is still 10 cm but the base is now 50 cm. The percentage decrease is 10/50 = 20%. Equal absolute differences can produce different percentage changes because their reference quantities differ.
## Two changes in sequence
Start with $100. Increase it by 20% to get $120. Then decrease the new amount by 20%, removing $24 and leaving $96. The increase and decrease do not cancel because the second 20% is a percentage of a different whole.

A useful representation is a chain of multipliers: 100 × 1.2 × 0.8 = 96. The final amount is 96% of the original, an overall decrease of 4%. In core work you may use a bar or unitary method instead; the essential idea is to name the whole at each stage.
## Find the missing part of a tank
A tank is 60% full. Adding 18 litres fills it completely. The added water represents 40% of the capacity. Ten percent is 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5 litres, so the full capacity is 45 litres. The starting water volume was 27 litres.

Check both the percentage and the physical total: 27 + 18 = 45 and 27/45 = 60%. A pupil who treats 18 litres as 60% will obtain a different answer by using the wrong correspondence. Write down the percentage that belongs to every given quantity.
## Guided task: compare claims
Two shops offer a discount of $12. At Shop A the original price is $60, and at Shop B it is $80. Shop A's discount is 20%; Shop B's is 15%. A larger percentage discount does not necessarily mean a larger dollar saving when original prices differ.

Now ask which shop has the lower final price: $48 versus $68. This is another question again. Keep 'discount amount', 'discount percentage' and 'price paid' as three distinct quantities. An advertisement may emphasise whichever looks most attractive.
## Explain and transfer
Explain to a partner why a 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease does not restore an amount. Use a simple starting value such as 20 and show the intermediate quantity. Then ask what percentage decrease would restore 30 to 20: the required decrease is one third of 30.

For independent work, name the 100% quantity, label the given amount's percentage, calculate one convenient portion, and verify forwards. These steps apply to population changes, capacity, prices and test-score comparisons without relying on a memorised story type.
