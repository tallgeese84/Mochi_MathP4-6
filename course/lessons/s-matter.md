# Matter: mass, space and states

## Matter has mass and occupies space
Matter includes air, water, rocks and living things. It has mass and occupies space. An object can be invisible and still be matter: clean air is a familiar example. Light and sound are not substances that fill a container in the same way; they are associated with energy transfer.

Mass describes the amount of matter in the primary model and is measured with a balance, commonly in grams or kilograms. Volume describes the space occupied, commonly in cubic centimetres or millilitres for small quantities. Mass and volume are different measurements, so never substitute one unit for the other.
## Solids
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume under ordinary conditions. A wooden block keeps its shape when placed in different containers. A flexible solid can bend under a force and still be a solid: flexibility does not make rubber a liquid.

A pile of sand can change its overall outline when poured, but individual grains are solids. Classify the material at the appropriate scale. If a solid is broken into pieces, each piece occupies space; breaking it does not automatically change its state of matter.
## Liquids
A liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of the part of its container that it occupies. Pour the same water into a tall narrow vessel and a short wide vessel: the water level changes, but the amount need not change if none is spilled or lost.

Read a measuring container at eye level using its scale correctly. A taller column does not by itself prove a larger volume. Geometry and measurement provide better evidence than appearance alone. Water can flow, but the ability to flow is not unique to liquids because gases also flow.
## Gases
A gas has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume: it spreads to fill the available container. Air in a balloon occupies space, and adding more air changes the amount of matter inside. Gas can escape through openings, so an apparently empty container may actually contain air.

A gas can be compressed much more readily than a liquid in simple classroom comparisons. Detailed particle arrangements are a useful later explanation, but are not required to describe the primary observations in this lesson. First make clear what the experiment actually shows.
## Worked example: trapped air
Push an inverted clear cup with dry tissue at its inner base straight down into water. In a careful demonstration, the trapped air prevents water from filling the entire cup, keeping the tissue dry. Tilt the cup and air bubbles can escape, allowing water farther in.

The explanation is that air occupies space, not that the cup contains “nothing”. A real test may fail if the cup tilts or the tissue hangs into water. Recognising such details helps distinguish an invalid idea from an imperfect setup.
## Measure with care
Compare equal water volumes in different unbreakable containers using a measuring jug. Record the volume before and after pouring, allowing for spills and drops left behind. Predict first whether changing the container changes the volume.

For an air demonstration, use only an adult-approved plastic syringe without a needle, and never point it at anyone. Do not heat or seal containers to investigate gas. The thinking goal is to relate resistance to compression and occupied space, not to create high pressure.
## Explain and transfer
Ask three separate questions: Does it have mass? Does it occupy space? Does its shape or volume depend on its container? These help organise evidence about matter and states.

Do not confuse a change of shape with a change of state. Bending wire, crushing a can and breaking ice are changes to objects; melting ice is a change from the solid state to the liquid state. The next lessons explain how heat is involved in such changes.
