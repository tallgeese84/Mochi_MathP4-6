# Forces: motion, shape and interactions

## A force is an interaction
A force is a push or pull exerted by one object on another. Forces can change an object’s speed, direction or shape. They do not always produce visible movement: pressing gently on a wall involves an interaction even if the wall does not noticeably move.

Identify both objects in the interaction. “There is a force” is less useful than “the hand pushes the trolley”. Then connect the force to the observed effect. Not every change has the same cause, so look carefully at the setup before naming a force.
## Contact and non-contact forces
Friction and the force from a compressed spring usually involve contact. Gravitational and magnetic forces can act without direct contact between the interacting objects. An object can experience several forces at the same time.

Primary questions focus on qualitative effects. Numerical acceleration, formal vector calculations and detailed resultant-force equations are extension topics, not assumed requirements here. A simple model should still be accurate: contact is not required for every force.
## Gravity and weight
Earth exerts gravitational force on objects, pulling them toward Earth. Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object. Mass and weight are different ideas even though everyday language sometimes mixes them. Mass is commonly measured in kilograms; force, including weight, is measured in newtons.

You do not need a weight formula for this primary lesson. Explain falling in terms of gravity, while recognising that air resistance or other forces can also act. The statement “heavier objects always fall faster” is not a valid general rule for every setup.
## Friction
Friction opposes relative sliding, or the tendency to slide, between surfaces in contact. It can slow a sliding object and produce heating. It can also be useful for grip, such as when a shoe grips the floor or a brake slows a wheel.

Avoid the over-broad rule “friction always acts opposite to the object’s motion” in every possible situation. Rolling and driven-wheel directions require more detailed analysis and are outside the primary friction-direction scope used here. For a block sliding across a fixed table, friction acts against its sliding motion.
## Springs and shape changes
Stretching or compressing a spring produces an elastic spring force tending to restore its shape. Within an appropriate working range, greater stretching usually produces a greater restoring force. Stretching too far can damage the spring, so the pattern must not be extended without limits.

A spring-based measuring device can compare forces after calibration. The app’s model is illustrative, not a calibrated instrument. Use only a safe classroom spring and light loads with an adult; never launch stretched objects toward people.
## Worked investigation: sliding
To investigate how surface type affects the distance a block slides, keep the block, starting conditions and measurement method the same. Use a repeatable release rather than an inconsistent hand push. Measure stopping distance and repeat trials.

If one surface gives a shorter stopping distance under those controls, greater resistance to sliding is a possible explanation. The test does not prove that every object on that surface behaves identically. Roughness, deformation and the contact pair all matter; state the observed comparison rather than a universal rule about appearance.
## Explain and transfer
For a forces explanation, identify the interacting objects, name the force, describe the relevant direction qualitatively, and connect it to change in motion or shape. Keep force and energy as related but different concepts.

Compare a falling ball, a sliding block, a compass needle and a stretched spring. Each involves interactions, but different forces dominate the explanation. A strong answer chooses the force from the evidence and describes its effect instead of selecting a familiar word at random.
