# Adding, subtracting and multiplying fractions

## Why common units matter
You would not add three metres to four centimetres and call the result seven metres. Fractions also need matching units. One third and one quarter are different-sized pieces. Rename them as fourths of thirds, or twelfths: 1/3 + 1/4 = 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12.

The denominator stays twelve when adding because the pieces remain twelfths. It does not become twenty-four. Use two equal paper strips to build the sum, then copy the idea onto a number line. The calculation should represent a total distance or amount, not just a manipulation of symbols.
## Add and subtract mixed amounts
To find 2 1/3 + 1 1/4, combine the whole numbers and then the fractions: 3 + 4/12 + 3/12 = 3 7/12. If the fraction sum reaches one, regroup it as another whole. Keep the equal-parts meaning visible throughout the calculation.

For 3 1/4 − 1 2/3, rename quarters and thirds as twelfths: 3 3/12 − 1 8/12. Regroup one whole from the first number: 2 15/12 − 1 8/12 = 1 7/12. Alternatively use improper fractions: 13/4 − 5/3 = 39/12 − 20/12 = 19/12. Both methods describe the same difference.
## Multiplication means part of a quantity
Find two thirds of three quarters of a rectangle. Shade three of four equal columns first. Then keep two of three equal rows within that shaded portion. Six of twelve equal small rectangles remain, so 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2.

The product can be smaller than either factor. Multiplying by a proper fraction scales a positive quantity down. Multiplying 3/4 by 2 makes two copies and gives 1 1/2; multiplying 3/4 by 1/2 gives half a copy, or 3/8. Multiplication describes scaling, not necessarily enlargement.
## Worked example in context
A recipe needs 2/3 cup of oats for one batch. For three batches, calculate 3 × 2/3 = 6/3 = 2 cups. For half a batch, calculate 1/2 × 2/3 = 1/3 cup. State which quantity is being scaled and by what factor.

Suppose you already have 1 1/4 cups and need 2 cups. The extra amount is 2 − 1 1/4 = 3/4 cup. Check by adding 1 1/4 and 3/4. A problem may combine multiplication and subtraction; each step should answer a named intermediate question.
## Diagnose an error
A pupil writes 2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7. Test it against a rough estimate: two thirds is already more than one half, but three sevenths is less than one half. Adding a positive amount cannot make that quantity smaller. The estimate reveals the error before a detailed correction.

For multiplication, a pupil writes 3/5 × 2/3 = 6/5 because 'the threes cancel'. Correct cancellation divides one numerator and one denominator by the same non-zero factor. Here the product is 6/15 = 2/5. Cancellation is a compact form of simplifying a product, not permission to erase arbitrary matching digits.
## Guided drawing and calculation
Draw a square and use different shading directions to show 3/4 of 2/3. Count the overlapping small regions and compare with 2/3 of 3/4. The overlap is one half in either order. Explain why the numerical result is the same, while the order of instructions in a story may differ.

Now solve 1 1/2 × 4 using both repeated addition and an improper fraction. Four copies of one and a half make six. The improper fraction calculation, 3/2 × 4 = 6, confirms it. Choose a method you can explain rather than always selecting the shortest-looking notation.
## Explain and transfer
Write a short rule in your own words for each operation. Addition and subtraction combine or compare equal-sized units. Multiplication scales a quantity or finds a fraction of it. This difference explains why common denominators are necessary for addition but not for the multiplication rule.

Before practice, predict whether your result should be above or below one, and above or below the starting amount. If a computed answer contradicts that prediction, revisit the model. A sensible estimate does not prove an answer, but it is an excellent error detector.
