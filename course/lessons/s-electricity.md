# Electric circuits: complete paths and comparisons

## A complete conducting path
A simple circuit needs a source such as a battery, connecting conductors and a component such as a bulb. For the bulb to light, there must be a complete conducting path through it between the battery terminals. A gap or an open switch breaks the path.

Touching one wire to a bulb is usually insufficient. Both contacts of the bulb must be connected appropriately in a complete path. Use the circuit diagram to trace the route continuously; an attractive picture without correct connections is not evidence of a working circuit.
## Conductors and insulators
Electrical conductors allow electric current to pass through readily. Metals such as copper are commonly used for wires. Electrical insulators, including the plastic coating on many wires, help prevent unintended conducting paths.

Heat conduction and electrical conduction are different properties, even though metals commonly do both well. Water near electrical equipment is hazardous. Use only a supervised low-voltage classroom kit, never mains sockets, damaged batteries or improvised connections that directly join battery terminals.
## Switches and current
Closing a switch completes its part of a conducting path; opening it breaks that path. Whether all bulbs turn off depends on where the switch is located. A switch in a shared part of a circuit can affect several branches, while one in a separate branch may affect only that branch.

Current is not “used up” by a bulb as it moves around a simple series circuit. Energy is transferred in components. At primary level, reason with complete paths, component arrangements and observed brightness rather than numerical current or resistance formulas.
## Series bulbs
In a series arrangement, components lie along one path. If that path is broken at one bulb, the other bulbs in the same path stop working. With comparable identical bulbs and the same suitable battery, adding bulbs in series commonly makes each bulb dimmer.

This brightness comparison depends on the specified components and working battery. Real bulbs and batteries are not perfect mathematical models. Do not claim a precise fraction of brightness without measurements, and do not assume all bulbs of different designs behave identically.
## Parallel bulbs
In a parallel arrangement, branches provide separate paths. If one bulb’s branch is broken, another complete branch can still work. Identical bulbs connected directly across the same suitable battery in separate branches can have similar brightness to a single bulb across it in the simple classroom model.

More parallel branches can make a real battery run down faster. The battery is not an unlimited source. A diagram must show where branches split and join; lines crossing on a page are connected only if the diagram indicates a junction.
## Worked investigation
Investigate the effect of adding a battery in series on one suitable bulb’s brightness using a rated classroom kit. Keep bulb type, wire connections and other conditions the same, and follow the kit’s allowed battery count. More batteries do not mean “always safer” or “always better”; too much can damage components.

For a bulb-arrangement comparison, use identical bulbs and the same supply conditions. Predict which paths remain complete when one branch switch opens. Then test in the app’s model or an adult-approved kit. A simulation is a simplified model, not a promise of exact real-world brightness.
## Explain and transfer
For any circuit, trace each complete path from one battery terminal, through the relevant components, to the other terminal. Identify the location of a break and which paths it interrupts.

Connect cause and observation: opening this branch switch breaks this bulb’s path, while the other branch remains complete. This is stronger than memorising “parallel is better”. Series and parallel arrangements serve different purposes; the reasoning comes from the actual connections.
