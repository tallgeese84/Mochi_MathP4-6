# Read data, calculate averages, question claims

## Begin with labels
A graph does not speak for itself. Read its title, category labels, units, scale and source. A bar reaching a height of four grid spaces might represent four objects, forty objects or another quantity depending on the scale. Check whether the axis begins at zero before comparing apparent differences.

Tables preserve exact values and make it easier to compare particular rows. Bar graphs compare categories; line graphs often show change across an ordered variable such as time. A pie chart represents parts of one whole. Choose a representation that matches the question rather than the most decorative option.
## Interpret a relationship
If a line graph shows a plant's height at weekly intervals, the vertical coordinate gives height and the difference between two coordinates gives growth over that interval. A steep segment suggests a larger change per horizontal interval only when the scales and intervals are comparable.

The segment between measured points does not prove exactly what happened at every moment. It is a visual connection or model. Do not infer a daily measurement from a graph that only records weekly heights unless interpolation is explicitly intended.
## Average is equal sharing of a total
For values 6, 8, 10 and 12, the total is 36 and the average is 36 ÷ 4 = 9. Imagine redistributing thirty-six counters into four equal groups. An average need not be one of the original observations.

The relationship can be reversed: total = average × number of data values. If five bags have an average mass of 240 g, their total mass is 1,200 g. This total is useful when a bag is added or removed; do not simply add a new measurement to the old average.
## Worked missing-value problem
Four quiz scores have an average of 15. Their total is 60. Three scores are 12, 16 and 14, totalling 42. The missing score is 60 − 42 = 18. Check that all four now sum to 60 and divide by four to recover 15.

If a fifth score of 20 is added, the new total is 80 and the new average is 80 ÷ 5 = 16. The average rises by one, not by five, because the new total is redistributed across five values. Tracking totals makes the reasoning visible.
## Combine groups correctly
One group of two pupils averages 10 points; another group of six averages 18 points. Their totals are 20 and 108, giving 128 points across eight pupils. The combined average is 16 points. Averaging 10 and 18 to get 14 would treat the differently sized groups as equal.

Averages can also hide variation. Two sets, 8 and 12 versus 0 and 20, both average ten, yet their values are spread differently. A fair description may need the individual values or their range as well as the average. An average alone cannot describe every feature of a data set.
## Read a pie chart critically
If one quarter of a pie chart represents 30 pupils, the complete group contains 120 pupils. Half of that circle represents 60 pupils. Use the stated fraction or clear labels; do not estimate a precise numerical fraction from a rough screen picture.

Two equally sized drawn pie charts may represent populations of different sizes. Half of a group of twenty is fewer people than a quarter of a group of one hundred. Compare the actual totals when the question asks for counts rather than proportions.
## Explain and transfer
Collect a small set of measurements, calculate the total and average, and draw one suitable graph. Ask a partner to describe a claim that the data supports and one that goes beyond it. A survey of friends may describe those friends without representing every child in Singapore.

Before practice, identify the population or set, read units and scale, and separate a value from a change in value. When using an average, keep the total and count visible. Good data reasoning includes noticing missing information and resisting conclusions the evidence cannot support.
