# Area measures inside; perimeter measures around

## Two measurements of one shape
A garden needs fencing around its edge and grass over its surface. Fencing depends on perimeter, measured in length units. Grass coverage depends on area, measured in square units. The same shape has both measurements, but they answer different questions.

Cover a rectangle with square tiles. If each row contains eight tiles and there are five rows, it covers forty unit squares. This explains area = length × breadth for a rectangle. Counting boundary edges explains perimeter = 2 × (length + breadth). State the unit before calculating.
## Recover a missing dimension
A rectangle has area 48 cm² and length 8 cm. Six rows of eight unit squares cover the rectangle, so the breadth is 48 ÷ 8 = 6 cm. Dividing area by a length recovers another length. Do not report six square centimetres as the breadth.

If a rectangle instead has perimeter 28 cm and length 8 cm, half the perimeter is 14 cm, equal to length plus breadth. Therefore breadth is 14 − 8 = 6 cm. The same answer can arise from different information, but the steps must match the measurement supplied.
## Why a triangle uses one half
Take two congruent right triangles and join them to make a rectangle. Each triangle occupies half the area of that rectangle. More generally, a triangle with base b and perpendicular height h has area b × h ÷ 2. The height measures the shortest distance from the opposite vertex to the line containing the base.

A sloping side is not automatically the height. For a triangle with base 10 cm and perpendicular height 6 cm, area is 30 cm². If the height falls outside an obtuse triangle, extend the base line mentally or on the given diagram. The perpendicular relationship still determines the height.
## Break a composite shape into parts
An L-shaped floor can be viewed as a large rectangle with a smaller rectangle removed. If the outer rectangle is 8 m by 6 m and the missing corner is 3 m by 2 m, the area is 48 − 6 = 42 m². Alternatively partition the remaining floor into two non-overlapping rectangles.

For perimeter, trace the actual exposed boundary. Internal dividing lines used in an area calculation are not fencing edges. Mark every boundary segment once. If a length is missing, deduce it from aligned totals, and explain the subtraction you use.
## Challenge an appealing rule
Two rectangles can have the same area but different perimeters. A 3 by 4 rectangle has area 12 and perimeter 14; a 2 by 6 rectangle has area 12 and perimeter 16. Equal coverage does not guarantee equal boundary length.

Likewise, doubling both dimensions multiplies area by four but perimeter by two. Explain this with rows and columns, not just by inserting numbers into formulae. The different scaling reveals why square units behave differently from ordinary length units.
## Guided paper activity
Draw a rectangle on squared paper and cut it along a diagonal. Rearrange the two triangles without stretching them. Their combined area remains unchanged because the same pieces are present. The external boundary may change when edges that were separate become joined.

Now draw two triangles on the same base with the same perpendicular height but different sloping sides. Their areas are equal. Use a ruler to compare heights and explain why appearance alone can make one seem larger. Measurements should come from stated dimensions or reliable construction.
## Explain and transfer
For each problem, say whether you are covering, enclosing or finding a dimension. Label square units for area and length units for perimeter. Before adding areas, ensure the pieces neither overlap nor leave gaps; before adding edge lengths, exclude internal lines.

A final explanation should connect the formula with rows, equal halves or decomposition. If you forget a formula, reconstruct it from those ideas. This makes unfamiliar composite shapes manageable without requiring a new rule for every outline.
