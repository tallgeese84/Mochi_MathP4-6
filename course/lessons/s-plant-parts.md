# Plant parts work together

## A plant is a system
A flowering plant has parts that work together. Roots, stems, leaves, flowers and fruits have different functions, although their shapes vary greatly among species. A useful labelled drawing shows relationships, not just a list of names.

Start with the question “What does this part help the plant do?” Then ask how it depends on other parts. Leaves need water supplied from the roots; roots need food made in green parts. Damage to one part can therefore affect the whole plant.
## Roots
Roots anchor the plant and absorb water and mineral salts from the surroundings. The water and dissolved mineral salts are transported to other parts. Some roots also store food. A root’s position underground is common but is not the definition of a root: some roots grow above ground.

Mineral salts are important nutrients, but they are not the plant’s food in the primary photosynthesis explanation. Green plants make food using light energy, water and carbon dioxide. Saying that roots “eat soil” hides this important distinction.
## Stems
Stems support leaves, flowers and fruits. They also contain transport pathways connecting the roots and other parts. Holding leaves in suitable positions can help them receive light. Some stems store food or have special forms.

A plant can have a soft stem or a woody stem. Size and stiffness vary, but the support and transport functions remain useful ideas. Do not identify every underground structure as a root merely because of its location; use a reference when identifying unfamiliar storage organs.
## Leaves
Green leaves are important sites of photosynthesis, the process through which plants use light energy to make food. Leaves also exchange gases with their surroundings. They are parts of a living system, not solar panels that directly store sunlight as a substance.

Leaf size, arrangement and structure affect how plants interact with their environment. At this stage, identify the function before learning detailed microscopic structures. The later photosynthesis lesson explains inputs, products and the difference between making food and releasing energy from food.
## Flowers, fruits and seeds
Flowers are involved in sexual reproduction in flowering plants. After successful fertilisation, seeds develop, and the flower’s ovary develops into a fruit in the usual primary model. Fruits protect seeds and can help their dispersal.

Flowers do not all have large colourful petals, and a mature plant may not be flowering when observed. Link the structure to its role and life cycle. Seeds contain a young plant and stored food that helps support early growth under suitable conditions.
## Worked system explanation
Suppose roots are severely damaged. Water uptake may decrease, reducing the water supply to leaves. Leaves may wilt and food-making can be affected. The explanation links damage, reduced function and consequences for other parts.

Do not conclude that every wilted plant has damaged roots. Lack of water, heat and other conditions can also contribute. Record observations and investigate possible causes. A system explanation is stronger when it distinguishes what is known from what is inferred.
## Draw, explain and transfer
Draw a familiar plant and label roots, stem, leaves and any reproductive parts visible. Beside each label, write one function. Add a sentence linking two parts, such as roots supplying water used by leaves.

Compare your drawing with a reliable plant reference and correct missing or misleading features. Before practice, explain why a plant grown with water and mineral salts still needs suitable light for sustained food production. This prepares you to reason about transport and photosynthesis together.
