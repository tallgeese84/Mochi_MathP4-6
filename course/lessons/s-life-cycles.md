# Life cycles: change and continuity

## A cycle links generations
An individual organism begins life, grows and develops, and eventually dies. A life cycle describes the sequence of stages through which a kind of organism passes, including reproduction that begins another generation. The individual does not return to being an egg; the next generation begins the cycle again.

A cycle diagram is a model. Its arrows show sequence, not equal time intervals. Different stages may last very different lengths of time. Label the stages and explain what the arrows mean before interpreting a diagram.
## Three-stage insect life cycles
Some insects, such as grasshoppers, have egg, nymph and adult stages. The nymph resembles the adult in several ways but is smaller and does not yet have the adult’s fully developed wings and reproductive structures. It grows through changes that include shedding its outer covering.

A nymph is not a miniature adult in every respect. Observe what remains similar and what changes. The three-stage description is useful for these insects, but it does not describe the life cycle of every animal with three stages in a simplified drawing.
## Four-stage insect life cycles
Butterflies have egg, larva, pupa and adult stages. A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly or moth. The larva feeds and grows; major changes occur during the pupal stage; the adult can reproduce. The pupa is living even though it may show little obvious movement.

The stages can differ greatly in appearance, food and habitat. This helps explain why the young and adult of one species may use different resources. Do not call every young insect a caterpillar: different insects have different larval forms and life cycles.
## Plants across a generation
A flowering plant can grow from a seed into a seedling and then a mature plant. Under suitable conditions, it produces flowers, followed by fruits and seeds after successful reproduction. Seeds may then germinate and start another generation.

Germination and reproduction are different processes. A seed has already formed through reproduction before it germinates. A seedling uses stored food initially; as green leaves develop, photosynthesis becomes important. Light requirements for germination vary, so do not assume every kind of seed must be exposed to light to begin germinating.
## Worked comparison
Compare a grasshopper and a butterfly. Both start as eggs and eventually become adults that reproduce. A grasshopper has a nymph stage; a butterfly has larval and pupal stages. The absence or presence of a pupal stage is a useful distinguishing feature.

Now compare a frog’s egg, tadpole and adult in a simplified cycle. A tadpole changes substantially as it develops. The fact that this simplified diagram has three stages does not make the frog an insect. Stage count and animal classification answer different questions.
## Observe a plant safely
Place a few suitable seeds on moist paper in a ventilated container, following an adult’s guidance. Keep them moist without submerging them. Record dates, root appearance and shoot growth. Handle soil hygienically and wash your hands. Do not eat experimental seeds or grow mouldy samples.

Draw observations at intervals with labels and a scale where possible. If one seed does not germinate, consider whether it was viable and whether conditions were suitable. One failure does not establish that the species never germinates in those conditions.
## Explain and transfer
When ordering life-cycle pictures, use evidence about structures and development rather than size alone. An adult of one species can be smaller than the young of another. Explain how reproduction connects the adult stage to a new generation.

For an unfamiliar organism, ask what observations would establish its stages. Long-term observation, photographs at known times and reliable reference sources can be combined. A complete cycle is a claim supported by evidence, not just a circle of attractive pictures.
