# Rates connect different units

## What does per mean?
A printer produces 18 pages per minute. The rate compares a number of pages with a duration. 'Per minute' means for each one minute under the conditions stated. A price of $3 per kilogram and a tap delivering 2 litres per minute have the same mathematical structure even though their units differ.

Write both units. Eighteen alone could mean pages, minutes or dollars. Eighteen pages per minute describes a relationship. In a constant-rate model, doubling the number of minutes doubles the pages printed. Real devices may pause or slow down, so a word problem must state or justify the constant-rate assumption.
## Build a rate table
If four notebooks cost $10 at a fixed price each, one notebook costs $10 ÷ 4 = $2.50. Three notebooks cost $7.50 and eight cost $20. Use a table with a column for notebooks and a column for total cost. Every row should preserve total cost divided by number of notebooks.

This is the unitary method: find the amount for one unit, then scale. Sometimes scaling directly is simpler. Twelve notebooks are three times as many as four, so they cost three times $10. Both methods depend on the same constant price relationship.
## Worked example with an unknown duration
A tap fills a container at 3 litres per minute. How long does it take to deliver 24 litres? Count the number of three-litre groups: 24 ÷ 3 = 8 minutes. Conversely, eight minutes at this rate gives 3 × 8 = 24 litres.

If the container already holds 6 litres and needs to reach 24 litres, only 18 litres must be added. The duration is 18 ÷ 3 = 6 minutes. A correct rate calculation can still answer the wrong question if you forget the starting amount.
## Compare options fairly
A 600 g packet costs $4.20 and a 1 kg packet costs $6.50. Convert both to a common mass unit before comparing prices. The smaller packet costs $4.20 ÷ 6 = $0.70 per 100 g. The larger costs $6.50 ÷ 10 = $0.65 per 100 g.

The larger packet has the lower unit price, but the better purchase may also depend on how much you need and whether any is wasted. Mathematics answers the stated comparison; a practical decision may use other relevant evidence. Explain the distinction instead of declaring that the largest packet is always best.
## Diagnose an assumption
A taxi fare contains a fixed starting charge plus a charge for distance. Doubling the distance need not double the total fare. This is not a constant total-cost-per-kilometre relationship. The starting charge remains the same while only one part scales.

Likewise, a worker who finishes one job in ten minutes may need rest or setup time before the next. Use a constant rate only when the problem gives that model. Spotting a missing assumption is a reasoning skill, not a reason to invent details that make the arithmetic convenient.
## Guided task
A machine packs 84 biscuits in seven minutes at a constant rate. First calculate 12 biscuits per minute. In 15 minutes it packs 180 biscuits. To pack 300 biscuits it takes 25 minutes. Verify each answer by returning to the same rate.

Now suppose a five-minute setup is needed before packing begins. Packing 300 biscuits still takes 25 active minutes, but the whole task takes 30 minutes. Draw a timeline separating setup from production. Naming the two kinds of time prevents a misleading average.
## Explain and transfer
Choose a rate you see at home: filling a bottle, paying for fruit or printing pages. Identify the two quantities and propose a measurement that could test whether the rate is reasonably constant. Record several pairs rather than relying on one observation.

Before practice, write the unknown's unit and decide whether you need to multiply or divide. Multiplication combines rate and number of units into a total; division can recover either missing quantity. Always test the proposed answer against the original relationship.
