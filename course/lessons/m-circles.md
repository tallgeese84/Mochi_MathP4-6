# Circles and composite boundaries

## Define the circle
A circle consists of points at the same distance from a centre. That distance is its radius. A diameter passes through the centre and joins two points on the circle, so its length is twice the radius. A chord joins two points on the circle but need not pass through the centre.

The circumference is the distance around the full circle. The area is the space enclosed. We use π to express the constant ratio of circumference to diameter. When a problem specifies 3.14 or 22/7 for π, use that value consistently rather than switching approximations mid-calculation.
## Connect the formulae to meaning
Circumference = π × diameter = 2πr. Area = πr², where r² means r × r. The units differ: circumference uses cm or m; area uses cm² or m². A formula written without its quantity and units is easy to misuse.

Doubling a radius doubles circumference but quadruples area. Both width and height of the covered region scale. This parallels the rectangle lesson and helps you predict the effect of changing a circular garden or a wheel's size.
## Worked full-circle example
A circle has radius 7 cm. Using π = 22/7, its circumference is 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44 cm. Its area is 22/7 × 7 × 7 = 154 cm². A diagram should identify the radius clearly so you do not substitute the 14 cm diameter into the area formula.

Check with scale: the circle fits inside a 14 cm by 14 cm square of area 196 cm². An answer much larger than 196 cm² cannot be its area. This bounding check helps catch the common radius-versus-diameter error.
## Semicircles and quarter circles
A semicircle's curved edge is half the full circumference, but its entire perimeter also includes one diameter. For radius 7 cm and π = 22/7, the curved edge is 22 cm and the perimeter is 22 + 14 = 36 cm. Its area is half the full area, 77 cm².

A quarter circle's perimeter contains one quarter of the circumference plus two radii. For the same radius, that is 11 + 7 + 7 = 25 cm. Draw a finger along the complete boundary to identify what is included before writing any formula.
## Composite figures
Imagine a semicircle attached along the 10 cm side of a rectangle. The shared diameter lies inside the combined shape and is not part of its external perimeter. The semicircle's radius is 5 cm because its diameter matches that rectangle side.

For area, add the rectangle and semicircle areas if they do not overlap except along the joining edge. For perimeter, add only the exposed rectangle edges and the semicircular arc. The same shared line can be essential for finding a radius while still being excluded from the outside boundary.
## Guided error analysis
A pupil calculates the perimeter of a quarter circle by dividing the full circumference by four. That finds the curved arc only. Ask them to show where they started tracing and whether the path returned to the starting point. The two radii complete the boundary.

Another pupil uses d² in the area formula. Since d = 2r, this makes the area four times too large. Compare the claimed area with an enclosing square. A geometric size check can reveal an algebraic substitution error without repeating the same calculation.
## Explain and transfer
Cut a paper disc into equal sectors and rearrange them to explore why circle area relates to a base near half the circumference and height near the radius. This is an approximation that improves with narrower sectors, not an exact rectangular dissection using only a few pieces.

Before independent practice, label radius and diameter, identify the requested measurement, state the π value and trace any composite boundary. Explain every included edge. A correct formula applied to the wrong boundary still gives the wrong answer.
