# Operations and the meaning of a method

## What are we trying to describe?
Addition joins amounts or extends a quantity. Subtraction can find a remainder or compare two quantities. Multiplication describes equal groups and scaling. Division can share a total into a known number of groups, or find how many groups of a known size fit. The same arithmetic can represent different stories, so name what every number means.

For 24 ÷ 6, one story shares 24 counters among six children and asks how many each receives. Another packs 24 counters into groups of six and asks how many groups there are. Both give 4, but the answer has a different meaning. Write the answer with its meaning, not as an isolated number.
## Build a reliable written method
For 327 × 24, partition 24 into 20 and 4. The first partial product is 6,540, and the second is 1,308. Their sum is 7,848. The second row in a written multiplication method represents tens, not another set of ones. A place-value chart explains its position.

For division, remember that each remainder still has a place value. Dividing 154 by 4, 1 hundred can be regrouped as 10 tens. After sharing tens and then ones, the result is 38 remainder 2. Verify 4 × 38 + 2 = 154. The remainder must be smaller than the divisor; otherwise another full group could still be made.
## Worked example with a decision
There are 154 pupils and each van holds 4 pupils. The arithmetic is 154 ÷ 4 = 38 remainder 2. We need 39 vans because two pupils still require places. If the story instead asks how many complete teams of four can be made, the answer is 38 teams with two pupils left over.

If 154 metres of ribbon are shared equally among four people, the remaining 2 metres can be divided: each receives 38.5 metres. A remainder does not prescribe a single response. Decide whether the objects can be split and whether every object must be accommodated.
## Order expresses structure
Consider 18 + 12 ÷ 3. Division acts on the 12, giving 18 + 4 = 22. In (18 + 12) ÷ 3, the entire sum is divided, giving 10. Brackets tell the reader which quantity is being treated as a group. They are part of the meaning, not decoration.

Multiplication and division have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right when no grouping says otherwise. Thus 24 ÷ 6 × 2 = 4 × 2 = 8. Addition and subtraction likewise share a priority. A mnemonic is only useful if it preserves these equal-priority rules.
## Find and repair a mistake
A pupil writes 36 − 8 + 2 = 36 − 10 = 26. The intended expression first removes eight and then adds two: 28 + 2 = 30. The pupil has silently inserted brackets to make 36 − (8 + 2). Explain this difference with counters or a number line.

Another pupil says multiplication always makes a number larger. It does when a positive quantity is multiplied by a whole number greater than one. Multiplying by one preserves it, and multiplying by zero gives zero. Later, multiplying by a proper fraction will scale it down. State the conditions of a rule.
## Guided practice on paper
A shop has 18 boxes containing 24 pencils each. It sells 75 pencils. Draw the relationship before calculating: total pencils minus sold pencils equals remaining pencils. Work 18 × 24 = 432, then 432 − 75 = 357. Check by adding back 75 to 357.

Try a second route: 20 × 24 − 2 × 24 − 75. Explain why subtracting two boxes corrects the temporary use of twenty boxes. A second method is especially valuable when it groups the calculation differently, because repeating the same mistaken steps may repeat the same error.
## Explain and transfer
Invent two stories for 35 ÷ 4 that need different final answers. Include a story about sharing a length and one about booking vehicles. Tell someone why a numerical calculation alone does not settle the practical question.

Before practice, make a habit of four checks: identify the relationship, estimate the scale, perform the calculation, and interpret the answer in the original setting. This routine is more reliable than searching the sentence for a word such as 'altogether' and automatically choosing an operation.
