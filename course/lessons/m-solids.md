# Symmetry, solids and nets

## Reflect rather than guess
A line of symmetry divides a figure so that reflection across the line maps the figure onto itself. Fold a paper shape along a proposed line: matching boundary points must coincide. Equal area on either side is necessary for many examples, but is not enough to establish symmetry.

A non-square rectangle has two lines of symmetry through the midpoints of opposite sides. Its diagonals are not reflection lines. A square has four: the two midlines and the two diagonals. Changing the shape's orientation on the page does not change its number of symmetry lines.
## Complete a reflection
On squared paper, each reflected point lies the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line on the opposite side. If a corner is three squares to the left of a vertical mirror, its image is three squares to the right at the same height.

Reflect the important corners first, then join them in the same order. For a sloping mirror line, distance must still be perpendicular to that line, not simply counted horizontally. A ruler and careful construction help when visual intuition becomes unreliable.
## Know the parts of a solid
A cube has six square faces, twelve edges and eight vertices. Each face meets neighbouring faces along edges; three edges meet at each vertex. A cuboid has the same counts, but its rectangular faces need not all be squares.

A triangular prism has two congruent parallel triangular end faces and three rectangular side faces. A square-based pyramid has a square base and four triangular faces meeting at a vertex. Cylinders and cones include curved surfaces, so do not treat every surface as a flat polygon face.
## From a solid to a net
A net is a connected flat arrangement of the faces that can fold into a solid without overlapping faces or leaving a missing face. Six squares alone do not guarantee a cube net. Their arrangement matters because folding may place two squares over the same face.

Choose one square as the base and imagine folding its neighbours up. Then track where the remaining squares go. Opposite cube faces do not share an edge after folding. Marking each square with a different colour or letter helps you track faces through the transformation.
## A worked folding strategy
For a strip of four equal squares, add one square above the second and one below the second. This is a valid cube net. The strip wraps around four side faces, while the two attached squares close the opposite ends. Build a paper model to test your mental prediction.

A straight strip of six squares is not a cube net: folding successive edges cannot close the solid into six distinct face positions. Explain the overlap or missing closure, rather than merely reporting that it 'looks wrong'. The exact interactive diagram uses six counted squares; decorative illustrations are not folding templates.
## Guided spatial investigation
Draw the valid six-square net described above. Before cutting, predict which faces will be opposite, then fold it and compare. If your prediction fails, trace the fold that changed your mental picture. The mismatch is useful evidence about the representation you used.

Next build a small cuboid from unit cubes. Draw what you see from the front, side and top. A single view can hide cubes behind others, so it may not determine the full solid. State when more than one arrangement fits the supplied views.
## Explain and transfer
Find a cardboard package and identify its solid shape. Carefully unfold a spare empty package with adult help for cutting, then distinguish structural faces from glue tabs. Tabs help assembly but do not count as additional faces of the mathematical solid.

Before practice, test symmetry by reflection and test nets by tracking actual face positions. These are transformation questions: ask what stays the same while the shape is reflected, rotated or folded. That habit is more dependable than memorising silhouettes.
