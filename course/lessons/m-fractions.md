# Fractions: name the whole

## A fraction needs a whole
A fraction describes a quantity relative to a chosen whole. If a ribbon is split into four equal lengths, each length is one quarter of that ribbon. Three such lengths make three quarters. The denominator tells the size of the equal parts; the numerator tells how many of those parts are counted.

Two children can each have half a cake and still have different amounts if their cakes differ in size. Before comparing fractions of physical objects, check that the wholes are equal. Before comparing abstract fractions as numbers, the unit whole is shared by definition. This small distinction matters in almost every fraction word problem.
## Equivalent names
Cut each third of a rectangle into two equal pieces. The whole now contains six equal pieces, and two thirds cover four of them. Thus 2/3 = 4/6. Both numerator and denominator have doubled because we changed the size of the pieces without changing the amount covered.

To simplify 12/18, divide both numbers by their common factor 6 to get 2/3. Dividing only the numerator changes the quantity. Explain equivalence by a diagram, by a number line, or by comparing both fractions with the same total. Simplifying is a change of name, not a reduction in the amount.
## Compare thoughtfully
For fractions with the same denominator, more parts mean more quantity: 5/8 > 3/8. For unit fractions with the same whole, more equal divisions make each part smaller: 1/8 < 1/3. Neither rule says that a larger denominator always means a smaller fraction when the numerator also changes.

To compare 3/4 and 5/6, rename them in twelfths: 9/12 and 10/12. Hence 5/6 is greater. You can also compare their gaps from one: 3/4 is missing 1/4, while 5/6 is missing only 1/6. A second representation gives you another way to check the reasoning.
## Worked example: beyond one
Seven quarters contain one full set of four quarters and three quarters left. Therefore 7/4 = 1 3/4. Conversely, 2 1/3 contains six thirds in its two wholes, plus one more third, giving 7/3. Draw separate wholes so that each is divided into the same-sized pieces.

An improper fraction is not an error. It is a convenient way of naming a quantity at least as large as one. Mixed numbers can be easier to interpret in a measurement story, while improper fractions often make multiplication easier. Choose the representation that makes the relationship clear.
## Fractions of a set
If three fifths of 25 counters are purple, partition the total into five equal groups. One fifth is 25 ÷ 5 = 5 counters. Three fifths are 3 × 5 = 15 counters. The denominator counts equal groups of the whole set, not individual counters.

Check with the remaining fraction: two fifths are 10 counters, and 15 + 10 = 25. If the result were not a whole number of counters, reconsider whether the problem permits splitting the objects. A fraction can describe a set even though the set consists of separate items.
## Use your notebook
Draw a number line from zero to two. Mark 1/2, 3/4, 5/4 and 1 1/2. Keep the scale constant on both sides of one. Explain why 5/4 and 1 1/4 occupy the same point. Then place 2/3 approximately and explain why it lies between one half and three quarters.

Make an intentionally misleading drawing in which three unequal pieces are labelled thirds. Ask another person to identify the mistake. Correct it by measuring or folding. A convincing-looking picture must still satisfy the definition of equal parts.
## Explain and transfer
When you encounter a fraction, ask: What is the whole? Are the parts equal? What does the numerator count? Could I locate the amount on a number line? These questions work for ribbon, money, collections and liquid measurements.

Before independent practice, explain why 2/5 and 4/10 are equal without simply saying 'multiply by two'. Your explanation should describe what happens to every part of the whole. That explanation will later justify fraction addition, multiplication and division.
